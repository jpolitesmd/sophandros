Phase I · Module I.2 · Six weeks · Layer 2

Homecoming

Return, guest-friendship, cunning, and a farmer’s account of work and justice.

Setting the scene

This layer adds mind and home to the foundation: the householder, Archaic age.

The second layer is the householder. The Iliad left a fighter on a field. The Odyssey asks whether that man can come home, and whether a stranger is safe in a house. Hesiod’s Works and Days puts the same person in a farmer’s year, under justice and work. Cunning and guest-friendship stand on the honor of Module 1. They do not replace it.

What failed, and what the next age learned. The household alone. Odysseus’ household nearly falls while he is away, and his boast to the Cyclops brings Poseidon’s anger. The Odyssey ends its cycle of revenge only because the gods step in, and Hesiod complains of crooked judgments by the powerful. A good household cannot protect itself from bad public justice. Households need a shared city and fair public law.

*Nostos* (homecoming), *xenia* (guest-friendship), *mētis* (cunning intelligence), identity and recognition, and, with Hesiod, work and justice.

The readings

The taught lesson, when it is written, is about half an hour on this site. The longer syllabus beside it — a language chapter, a double translation — is optional. You do not need it to read the lesson.

  1. Reading 1 Telemachus and the Suitors

    Read

    Odyssey Books 1–2: the proem (1.1–10), Athena as Mentes, the suitors, the assembly at Ithaca. Skim Book 4.1–624, Telemachus at Sparta with Helen and Menelaus.

    Look

    Set the two proems side by side, Iliad 1.1–7 against Odyssey 1.1–10. Wrath, then the man of many turns.

    Questions

    • What does a household look like without its master?
    • Telemachus has to grow up. What does Athena teach him?
    • Set this opening next to the opening of the Iliad. What has changed?

  2. Reading 2 Calypso and the Phaeacians

    Read

    Odyssey Book 5 (Hermes to Calypso, the raft, the storm), Book 6 (Nausicaa), and Book 8.1–103 and 8.471–586 (Demodocus, and Odysseus weeping).

    Look

    The Odyssey Landscapes, Roman wall paintings of the 1st century BC from a house on the Esquiline in Rome, found in 1848 in Via Graziosa (now Via Cavour). They are in the Vatican Museums, in the Room of the Aldobrandini Wedding.

    Questions

    • Odysseus refuses Calypso’s offer of immortality (5.203–24). Why?
    • Nausicaa, and the ethics of hospitality to a stranger.
    • Why does Odysseus weep at a song about his own deeds?

  3. Reading 3 The Cyclops and Circe

    Read

    Odyssey Book 9 (the Lotus-eaters, Polyphemus, “Nobody,” and the boast at 9.502–05) and Book 10 (Aeolus, the Laestrygonians, Circe).

    Look

    The Eleusis Amphora (c. 670–650 BC, Protoattic), the blinding of Polyphemus, and the Sperlonga sculpture group of the same episode.

    Questions

    • Polyphemus violates xenia, and so, arguably, does Odysseus. Who is the guest and who the host?
    • Why does Odysseus reveal his name (9.502–05), and what does it cost him?
    • Cunning (mētis) against strength: which does the poem prize?

  4. Reading 4 The Dead and the Sirens

    Read

    Odyssey Book 11, the Nekyia: Elpenor, Tiresias, Anticleia, Agamemnon, Achilles (11.467–540), and Ajax’s silence (11.543–67). Book 12: the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, the cattle of the Sun.

    Look

    The Siren Painter’s stamnos, Attic red-figure, c. 480–470 BC: Odysseus bound to the mast. British Museum 1843,1103.31 (old catalogue E440), from Vulci.

    Questions

    • Achilles in the underworld says he would rather be a hired labourer than king over all the dead (11.489–91). Does this overturn the Iliad?
    • The Sirens offer knowledge. Why must Odysseus be bound?
    • The crew eat the cattle of the Sun. Who is responsible?

  5. Reading 5 Recognition and Revenge

    Read

    Odyssey Book 19 (Penelope and the beggar; Eurycleia and the scar, 19.386–475), Book 21 (the bow), and Book 22 (the slaughter of the suitors).

    Look

    The Penelope Painter’s skyphos: the nurse recognising Odysseus by the scar, and Penelope seated in the classic pose.

    Questions

    • Erich Auerbach’s essay “Odysseus’ Scar” (Mimesis, 1946) contrasts Homer’s style with the Bible. Is the digression a flaw, or the point?
    • Is the slaughter in Book 22 justice, or excess?
    • Who recognises Odysseus, and how: the dog, the nurse, the son, the wife?

  6. Reading 6 Home, and the Farmer’s Year

    Read

    Odyssey Book 23 (Penelope tests Odysseus; the bed, 23.177–230) and Hesiod, Works and Days 1–382: the two Strifes (11–26), Prometheus and Pandora (42–105), the five ages (106–201), justice (202–285), and work.

    Look

    A medieval manuscript of Works and Days, and the question of how the text reached us.

    Questions

    • The bed is rooted in the olive tree. What does it say about the marriage and the house?
    • Hesiod’s ages of man, and “work is no disgrace” (311). How does a farmer’s ethic differ from a hero’s?
    • Compare Homer’s gods and Hesiod’s gods on justice.

Mastery check

Pass this to unlock the next module.

  1. A comprehension quiz of 20 questions on Odyssey Books 1, 5, 6, 9–12, 19, and 21–23, and on Works and Days 1–382. Terms include nostos, xenia, mētis, kleos, polytropos, and dikē. Pass mark 80 percent.
  2. Latin: 25 items from chapters 7–12, plus five review items from chapters 1–6. Pass mark 80 percent.
  3. An unseen double translation at the level of chapters 1–12, in both directions, compared with the original. Tutor review is optional.
  4. Recitation: Odyssey 1.1–10 in English, or in transliterated Greek, plus six to eight lines of your Latin text.
  5. A commonplace essay of 600 words: “Achilles or Odysseus: which is the better model for a modern professional?” Cite both epics by book and line.
  6. A sketchbook with three drawings: the Polyphemus amphora, Odysseus at the mast, and your own map of the wanderings.
