Phase I · Module I.5 · Six weeks · Layer 5

The Examined Life

Socratic questioning, the just city, and proof as a discipline.

Setting the scene

This layer adds the examined mind to the foundation: socrates, and a mind that can prove, Socrates, Plato, Euclid.

The fifth layer is a mind that examines itself. The syllabus puts the line from the Apology here: the unexamined life is not worth living. The readings are Euthyphro, Apology, Crito, Phaedo, Meno, and the Republic, with Euclid’s Elements, Book I, as the discipline of proof. The earlier layers gave the man a prize, a house, a city, and a court. This one asks him to say what he knows.

What failed, and what the next age learned. The city kills its philosopher. Athens executed Socrates in 399 BC, and the Thirty Tyrants included men linked to his circle, such as Critias. Inquiry could unsettle citizens without making them better. Plato’s ideal city was never achieved. The traditional account of his attempts to advise Syracuse rests partly on the Seventh Letter, whose authenticity is disputed. Reason must become practical wisdom, fitted to real men and cities.

“The unexamined life is not worth living” (*Apology* 38a). Socratic questioning, piety and justice, the soul, knowledge and recollection, the just city, the philosopher and the Cave, and proof as an intellectual discipline, in Euclid.

Summary of the drafted syllabus. The readings below are the ones named there.

From the text

Short passages from this module’s readings, in public-domain translations.

Plato, five dialogues
Euthyphro, Apology, Crito, Meno, and Phaedo, in G. M. A. Grube, Five Dialogues (Hackett; 2nd ed., revised by John M. Cooper). Stephanus page numbers are standard across editions.
Republic
Allan Bloom (Basic Books), or G. M. A. Grube revised by C. D. C. Reeve (Hackett). Also Plato: Complete Works, edited by John M. Cooper (Hackett).
Euclid
Elements, Book I. T. L. Heath (Dover, or the Green Lion Press one-volume edition edited by Dana Densmore). Oliver Byrne’s coloured Euclid of 1847 is optional, and is in the public domain.
Latin
Wheelock chapters 25–30, or Lingua Latina caps. XXV–XXX.
Greek
Athenaze Book I, chapters 6–8. Graded reading from Plato begins, with phrases from the Apology, including 38a: the unexamined life.

The readings

The taught lesson, when it is written, is about half an hour on this site. The longer syllabus beside it — a language chapter, a double translation — is optional. You do not need it to read the lesson.

  1. Reading 1 Socrates on Trial

    Read

    Euthyphro in full (the dilemma at 10a: is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious?). Apology in full: the oracle and Socratic ignorance (20e–23c), the gadfly (30e), the unexamined life (38a), the closing words (42a).

    Look

    The Ancient Agora of Athens. The Stoa of Attalos, reconstructed in 1953–56, the law courts, and the kleroterion, the allotment machine for jurors.

    Questions

    • The Euthyphro dilemma. Does it apply to a professional code, right because the code says so?
    • Socratic ignorance. What would knowing that you do not know look like on a ward round?
    • Was Socrates guilty as charged?

  2. Reading 2 The Laws and the Death of Socrates

    Read

    Crito in full, including the speech of the Laws (50a–54d). Phaedo: the opening (57a–69e, philosophy as practice for dying) and the final argument and death scene (115b–118a), “Crito, we owe a cock to Asclepius” (118a).

    Look

    Jacques-Louis David, The Death of Socrates (1787), the Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York.

    Questions

    • Should Socrates have escaped? Weigh the argument of the Laws.
    • For physicians: the Phaedo’s description of hemlock (117e–118a), and the cock for Asclepius, the god of healing. What does Socrates mean?
    • David’s painting. What does it add to Plato, and what does it falsify?

  3. Reading 3 Learning as Recollection, and Euclid I

    Read

    Plato, Meno, in full: Meno’s paradox (80d–e), the slave-boy’s geometry lesson (82b–85b), true opinion against knowledge (97a–98a). Euclid, Elements I: definitions, postulates, common notions; Proposition 1 (the equilateral triangle), Proposition 5 (the pons asinorum), and Proposition 47 (Pythagoras).

    Look

    The papyrus fragment P. Oxy. I 29, a piece of Euclid’s Elements from Oxyrhynchus, and Oliver Byrne’s coloured Euclid (1847).

    Do

    Construct Elements I.1 with compass and straightedge, and work the doubled square from the Meno yourself.

    Questions

    • Is learning recollection, or something else?
    • What does a proof give that experience cannot, and the reverse (cf. evidence-based medicine)?

  4. Reading 4 What Is Justice?

    Read

    Republic I: Cephalus, Polemarchus, and Thrasymachus, “justice is the advantage of the stronger” (338c), and the craft analogy, the doctor who serves the patient (341c–342e). Republic II to 369b: Glaucon’s challenge, the Ring of Gyges (359c–360d), Adeimantus.

    Look

    A bust of Plato, a Roman copy of the portrait attributed to Silanion, Capitoline Museums, Rome (MC 1377).

    Questions

    • For physicians: Socrates argues that the doctor, as doctor, seeks the patient’s good and not his own fee (341c–342e). Is that true of medicine today?
    • Would you be just with the Ring of Gyges?
    • Compare Thrasymachus with the Athenians at Melos, in Module 3.

  5. Reading 5 The Soul and the City

    Read

    Republic IV, 427d–445e: the four virtues, the tripartite soul (reason, spirit, appetite), Leontius and the corpses (439e–440a), justice as the health of the soul (444c–e). Republic V, 473c–480a: philosopher-kings, the third wave (473c–d).

    Look

    Return to the kleroterion. Lottery democracy, set against rule by philosopher-kings.

    Questions

    • Justice as the health of the soul (444c–e). How good is the medical analogy?
    • Leontius: when have your reason and your appetite fought?
    • Should experts rule?

  6. Reading 6 The Cave

    Read

    Republic VI, 507b–511e, the Sun and the Divided Line. Republic VII, 514a–521b, the Allegory of the Cave, and 521c–531c, the curriculum of arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, and harmonics, which this course itself echoes.

    Look

    Jan Saenredam after Cornelis van Haarlem, Plato’s Cave, an engraving of 1604. Impressions are in the National Gallery of Art, Washington, and the Rijksmuseum.

    Questions

    • Who are the prisoners today, and what are the shadows?
    • Why must the philosopher go back down into the cave?
    • Plato’s curriculum in Book VII, set next to ours. What is mathematics for, in an education?

Mastery check

Pass this to unlock the next module.

  1. A comprehension quiz of 20 questions on Euthyphro, Apology, Crito, the death scene of the Phaedo, the Meno, and Republic I, II, IV, V, VI, and VII. Terms include elenchus, aretē, psychē, anamnēsis, eidos or Form, and dikaiosynē, plus the Ring, the Line, and the Cave. Pass mark 80 percent.
  2. Latin: 25 items from chapters 25–30, plus five review items. Pass mark 80 percent.
  3. Greek: 20 items from Athenaze chapters 1–8, and a word-by-word translation of Apology 38a. Pass mark 80 percent.
  4. An unseen Latin double translation through chapter 30, plus one Greek sentence in both directions.
  5. Geometry: photographed constructions of Euclid I.1 and a proof of I.47, or of the doubling in the Meno, with a written justification of each step.
  6. Recitation: Apology 38a in Greek and in English, and the opening of the Cave (514a) in English.
  7. A commonplace essay of 600 words: “The doctor as doctor: Socrates’ craft argument (Republic 341c–342e) and the ethics of modern medicine.”
  8. A sketchbook with three drawings: the kleroterion, the Cave after Saenredam, and your own Divided Line.
