Phase I · Module I.6 · Six weeks, plus a capstone week · Layer 6

Virtue and Polity

Happiness, habit, practical wisdom, and the proportions of the Parthenon. The phase closes here.

Setting the scene

This layer adds practical wisdom to the foundation: the complete man, Aristotle and the Parthenon.

The sixth layer is practical wisdom, and it is meant to finish the Greek figure. Aristotle’s Ethics treats happiness as an activity of the soul in accordance with virtue, and virtue as a mean formed by habit. The Politics calls the human being a political animal. The same module looks at the Parthenon, which the syllabus dates 447–432 BC, as a civic statement in stone. Honor, home, the citizen, the court, and the examined mind are supposed to be in him already.

What failed, and what the next age learned. The polis falls. The Greek cities kept fighting one another and could not unite. Philip defeated Athens and Thebes at Chaeronea in 338 BC, and after Alexander’s death in 323 BC his generals split the empire. Aristotle described the polis as the natural home of the good life just as it lost its independence. Virtue in persons and small cities was not enough without durable institutions, law, and unity across a large state, which Rome attempted.

Happiness (*eudaimonia*) as an activity of the soul in accordance with virtue. Virtue as a mean formed by habit. Practical wisdom (*phronēsis*). The human being as a political animal. The best practicable constitution. Aristotle the biologist. Proportion in the Parthenon.

Summary of the drafted syllabus. The readings below are the ones named there.

The readings

The taught lesson, when it is written, is about half an hour on this site. The longer syllabus beside it — a language chapter, a double translation — is optional. You do not need it to read the lesson.

  1. Reading 1 The Good Life

    Read

    Nicomachean Ethics I. The highest good (1094a1–1095a13); the three lives (1095b14–1096a10); the function argument (1097b22–1098a20); happiness and fortune; Priam (1100a5–9, 1101a6–8).

    Look

    Raphael’s School of Athens (1509–11), Stanza della Segnatura, Vatican. Plato points up. Aristotle holds the Ethics and gestures level with the earth.

    Questions

    • What is the ergon, the function, of a physician, and of a human being?
    • Can bad luck, Priam’s luck, destroy a happy life?
    • Compare Solon speaking to Croesus in Module 3: call no man happy until he is dead.

  2. Reading 2 Virtue as a Mean, Formed by Habit

    Read

    Nicomachean Ethics II: virtue comes from habituation, “we become just by doing just acts” (1103a14–b25); the doctrine of the mean (1106a26–1107a27); the table of virtues and vices (1107a28–1108b10). Ethics III, 1115a6–1117b22, on courage.

    Look

    The Lyceum archaeological site in Athens, where Aristotle taught.

    Questions

    • For physicians: Aristotle often uses medicine as his model, as with diet and exercise in the mean (1104a11–27). Is clinical judgement a virtue in his sense?
    • Which virtue in the table is hardest for you?
    • Courage in a hospital. What is the mean between cowardice and rashness?

  3. Reading 3 Practical Wisdom and the Highest Life

    Read

    Nicomachean Ethics VI: the intellectual virtues, and phronēsis (1140a24–b30; 1141b8–1142a30, particulars and experience). Ethics X, 1177a12–1179a32, contemplation as the happiest life, and 1179a33–1181b23, where ethics needs laws and turns toward the Politics.

    Look

    Return to The School of Athens. The two gestures are two answers to the question of which life is best.

    Questions

    • Why can the young not have phronēsis (1142a11–20)? What does that say about a medical residency?
    • Contemplation against action. Does Book X contradict the rest of the Ethics?
    • Why does ethics end in law?

  4. Reading 4 The Political Animal and the Constitutions

    Read

    Politics I.1–2 (1252a1–1253a39): “man is by nature a political animal” (1253a2–3), speech and justice. Politics III.6–9 (1278b6–1281a10): the six constitutions, correct and deviant. III.11 (1281a40–b15): the wisdom of the many.

    Look

    The Parthenon (447–432 BC) as a civic statement, and the Panathenaic frieze, in the British Museum and the Acropolis Museum.

    Questions

    • Is a human being outside the city “a beast or a god” (1253a29)?
    • Where does the constitution of the United States sit in Aristotle’s table?
    • The wisdom of the many. Does a committee decide better than an expert?

  5. Reading 5 The Middle Constitution, and Aristotle the Biologist

    Read

    Politics IV.11 (1295a25–1296b2), the middle class and the best practicable constitution, and IV.8–9, polity as a mixture. Parts of Animals I.5 (644b22–645a36): there is something wonderful in all natural things, and the story of Heraclitus at the oven (645a17–23).

    Look

    Stuart and Revett, The Antiquities of Athens: engraved views and measured drawings of the Parthenon, including the eastern side drawn in 1751. Those drawings shaped Greek Revival architecture, and they bear on the Founders Edition.

    Questions

    • For physicians: Aristotle defends the study of the lowlier animals and their tissues, disgust included (645a26–36). Is this medicine’s founding apology?
    • Why should a large middle class make for stability?
    • Is the mixed constitution, which Phase II will meet in Polybius, already here?

  6. Reading 6 Proportion and the Parthenon

    Read

    Pollitt, Art and Experience in Classical Greece, on the High Classical period and the Parthenon. The exact chapters are still to be set. Also Vitruvius, De architectura III.1, on symmetry and the human body, a preview of Phase II.

    Look

    The Parthenon’s refinements: entasis, the upward curvature of the stylobate, the inward tilt of the columns, the corner contraction. Measure and sketch the façade from a photograph.

    Questions

    • Aristotle’s mean, set next to the Parthenon’s proportions. Is beauty a kind of virtue?
    • Why would builders deliberately correct what the eye sees?
    • What is the single most important idea of the Greek phase for your own life?

Mastery check

Pass this to unlock the next module.

  1. A comprehension quiz of 20 questions on Ethics I, II, VI, and X, Politics I, III, and IV, and Parts of Animals I.5. Terms include eudaimonia, ergon, aretē, mesotēs, hexis, phronēsis, theōria, polis, and politeia. Pass mark 80 percent.
  2. Latin: 25 items from Wheelock chapters 31–36, or from Familia Romana XXXI–XXXV, plus five review items. Pass mark 80 percent.
  3. Greek: 20 items from Athenaze chapters 1–11, and a translation of Ethics 1098a16 or Politics 1253a2–3. Pass mark 80 percent.
  4. An unseen Latin passage, double-translated, and one Greek sentence in both directions.
  5. A commonplace essay of 600 words: “Phronēsis at the bedside: Aristotle’s practical wisdom and clinical judgement.”
  6. A sketchbook: the Doric order, labelled; the Parthenon façade; a vase or sculpture of your choice.

Phase I capstone

Week 37, about five or six hours, required to complete Phase I. The aim is to pull the Greek phase together, in the spirit of Milton’s advice in Of Education to “retire back… into the rear of what they have been taught.”

  1. A translation portfolio: your best Ascham double translations from Modules 1–6, with the phrase notebook (propria, translata, synonyma, diversa, contraria, phrases). Include at least one Greek sentence.
  2. A capstone oration or essay of 1,200–1,500 words, recorded if you choose the oration: “The best life and the best city: from Homer to Aristotle.” Trace one idea, honour, justice, wisdom, or the good life, through at least four authors from the phase. Cite them by book and line, or by Stephanus or Bekker number.
  3. A recorded recitation: Iliad 1.1–7 or Odyssey 1.1–10 in English, one Greek line of your choice (for example Apology 38a or Antigone 332), and eight to ten lines of Latin.
  4. A sketchbook review: six sketches from the phase.
  5. Optional, on the tutored tier: a 45-minute oral examination on the portfolio, in a Socratic style.

Athens and Delphi, after the reading

The optional Athens and Delphi journey comes after the capstone, so every site is one you have already read. Milton, in Of Education, sends the traveller abroad “not to learn Principles but to enlarge Experience.” About seven to ten days, with a specialist operator.

Suggested Athens and Delphi itinerary
When Site Read on site
Days 1–2 The Acropolis and the Acropolis Museum: the Parthenon, the frieze, the refinements. Thucydides 2.35–46, the Funeral Oration, and your Module 6 sketches.
Day 3 The Ancient Agora: the Stoa of Attalos museum (the kleroterion, ostraca), the Royal Stoa, the Pnyx, the Areopagus. The opening of the Euthyphro, Apology 38a, and Eumenides 681–710.
Day 4 The Theatre of Dionysus, the Lyceum site, and the National Archaeological Museum (the Mask of Agamemnon). Antigone 332–375, Ethics I, and Iliad Book 1.
Day 5 Marathon, the Soros. Herodotus 6.111–17.
Days 6–7 Delphi: the Temple of Apollo, the theatre, the Tholos, and the Delphi Museum, including the Charioteer. Herodotus 1.46–56, Croesus and the oracle; the opening of the Eumenides; “Know thyself.”
Day 8, optional Epidaurus: the theatre, and the sanctuary of Asclepius. Oedipus the King 1–150, and Phaedo 118a.
Day 9, optional Mycenae, the Lion Gate. Agamemnon 905–974, and Iliad 1.
