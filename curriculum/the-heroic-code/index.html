Phase I · Module I.1 · Six weeks · Layer 1

The Heroic Code

Honour, glory, wrath, mortality, and where the heroic code gives out.

Setting the scene

This layer adds honor to the foundation: the warrior, Bronze Age.

This module is the warrior, before the course has a household, a city, or a court. The reading is the Iliad. Worth, in that camp, is public: a prize the army can see, and a song that can outlive the man. Mycenae is the citadel the later tradition gave to Agamemnon. The Lion Gate is part of the UNESCO listing “Archaeological Sites of Mycenae and Tiryns.” The gold mask Schliemann called Agamemnon’s is from Grave Circle A, about 1550–1500 BC, centuries older than the Trojan War as that later tradition told it. The name is his. The poem is the lesson, not the mask.

What failed, and what the next age learned. Heroic wrath. Homeric honor depended on other men’s opinion and on personal revenge. Achilles’ wrath cost the Greeks many lives and led him to abuse Hector’s body. A code built only on reputation could not settle quarrels or set limits. Courage needs self-command and judgment, which the Odyssey adds.

Honour (*timē*), glory (*kleos*), wrath (*mēnis*), mortality, and the limits of the heroic code.

The readings

The taught lesson, when it is written, is about half an hour on this site. The longer syllabus beside it — a language chapter, a double translation — is optional. You do not need it to read the lesson.

  1. Reading 1 Laying the first stone: honor and glory

    Read

    Iliad Book 1: the quarrel of Agamemnon and Achilles, Chryses, Thetis and Zeus. The guide is the proem (1.1–7) and the meaning of mēnis.

    Look

    The Lion Gate at Mycenae, the citadel of Agamemnon’s legend, part of the UNESCO listing “Archaeological Sites of Mycenae and Tiryns.” Sketch the relieving triangle above the lintel.

    Read the taught lesson The lesson explains the reading and answers the lines below. In these early weeks those lines are not set as open work.

    Questions

    • Is Achilles right to withdraw? What is timē worth?
    • Agamemnon is king, Achilles is the better fighter: where does authority come from?
    • Whom in your own working life do you recognise in this quarrel?

  2. Reading 2 Honor in a society of chieftains

    Read

    Iliad Book 2.1–483 (Agamemnon’s dream, the testing of the army, Thersites). Skim the Catalogue of Ships (2.484 to the end). Book 3 in full: Helen on the wall, and the duel of Paris and Menelaus.

    Look

    The “Mask of Agamemnon,” National Archaeological Museum, Athens, from Grave Circle A at Mycenae, c. 1550–1500 BC, centuries older than the Trojan War tradition.

    Read the taught lesson The lesson explains the reading and answers the lines below. In these early weeks those lines are not set as open work.

    Questions

    • Why does Homer give Thersites, a common soldier, a voice?
    • Helen watching from the wall: what is the war for?
    • Schliemann named the mask after Agamemnon. What do we want from relics?

  3. Reading 3 Honor tied to home and duty

    Read

    Iliad Book 6 (Glaucus and Diomedes, 6.119–236; Hector and Andromache, 6.390–502) and Book 9 (the embassy to Achilles, Phoenix, and Achilles’ speech, 9.307–429).

    Look

    Exekias, Achilles and Ajax playing a board game, a black-figure amphora of about 540–530 BC, Vatican Museums, Museo Gregoriano Etrusco, inv. 16757.

    Read the taught lesson The lesson explains the reading and answers the lines below. In these early weeks those lines are not set as open work.

    Questions

    • Hector chooses duty over family. Is he right?
    • Achilles in Book 9 questions the whole heroic bargain (“equal honour for the coward and the brave”). Does he have a point?
    • Glaucus says the generations are like leaves. What does that do to the idea of glory?

  4. Reading 4 Loyalty and the whole world of men

    Read

    Iliad Book 16, the Patrocleia: Patroclus fights in Achilles’ armour, Sarpedon, the death of Patroclus. Book 18: Achilles’ grief, Thetis, and the Shield of Achilles (18.478–608).

    Look

    The Euphronios Krater (c. 515 BC), on which Sleep and Death carry the body of Sarpedon (Iliad 16.666–83). It is in the National Archaeological Museum of Cerveteri, and was lent to the Acropolis Museum in Athens for June–August 2026. Also the Shield as an ekphrasis: sketch its concentric scenes from 18.478–608.

    Read the taught lesson The lesson explains the reading and answers the lines below. In these early weeks those lines are not set as open work.

    Questions

    • Why does the shield show a city at peace, with weddings and harvests, in the middle of a war poem?
    • Achilles’ grief: what does it reveal that his anger hid?

  5. Reading 5 The limits of honor

    Read

    Iliad Book 22 in full: Hector before the walls, the chase, Athena’s deception, Hector’s death, Andromache’s lament. Book 19, the reconciliation, is optional.

    Look

    Return to the Lion Gate and the citadel walls. Compare “the walls of Troy” in Book 22 with Bronze Age fortification, and sketch a section of Cyclopean masonry.

    Read the taught lesson The lesson explains the reading and answers the lines below. In these early weeks those lines are not set as open work.

    Questions

    • Hector’s monologue (22.99–130): when does courage become pride?
    • Achilles refuses to bargain over the body. What has the code become?
    • Andromache’s lament: whose story does the poem end up telling?

  6. Reading 6 Honor completed by compassion

    Read

    Iliad Book 24 in full: the ransom of Hector, Priam in Achilles’ hut (24.468–676), the jars of Zeus (24.527–33), and the funeral of Hector.

    Look

    The Brygos Painter’s “Ransom of Hector” skyphos, c. 490 BC, from Cerveteri. Kunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna, Antikensammlung inv. IV 3710, currently shown at Schloss Ambras, Innsbruck.

    Read the taught lesson The lesson explains the reading and answers the lines below. In these early weeks those lines are not set as open work.

    Questions

    • Why does the Iliad end with a funeral, and not with the fall of Troy?
    • Priam kisses “the hands that killed my son.” What does the poem hold up as the highest human act?
    • Write a definition of the heroic code in about a hundred words, and say where it fails.

Mastery check

Pass this to unlock the next module.

  1. A comprehension quiz of 20 questions on Books 1, 3, 6, 9, 16, 18, 22, and 24, including mēnis, timē, kleos, xenia, aristeia, and ekphrasis. Pass mark 80 percent.
  2. Latin vocabulary and forms: 25 items from chapters 1–6 of the textbook you chose. Pass mark 80 percent.
  3. An Ascham double translation: a short unseen Latin passage at the level of chapters 1–6, into English and back, compared with the original. A tutor may review it. That review is optional.
  4. Recitation: Iliad 1.1–7 in English, or in transliterated Greek, plus four to six lines of your Latin chapter.
  5. A commonplace essay of 500 words, “Is the heroic code worth living by?”, citing at least three passages by book and line.
  6. A sketchbook with three drawings: the Lion Gate, the layout of the shield, and one vase.
