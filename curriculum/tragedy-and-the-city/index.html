Tragedy and the City

Justice, the family, and the city, staged at public expense.

Setting the scene

This layer adds conscience and law to the foundation: the person before a court, Tragedy and the courts.

The fourth layer is conscience, and a law that can answer it. The syllabus week on the Eumenides is titled “the birth of the court.” Antigone sets a burial against a ruler’s order. Oedipus the King and Medea are in the same module. Honor told a warrior what his prize was worth. A court asks what he owes when the prize and the house and the city disagree.

What failed, and what the next age learned. Unresolved conflict. Tragedy staged the collisions of law, family, and conscience, but Athens’ courts and assembly still made grave errors, such as the execution of the generals after Arginusae in 406 BC. Showing a problem on stage did not supply a reasoned standard of justice. Conscience needs examined reasons, which Socrates demands.

Tragedy as a civic institution, the City Dionysia. Justice and revenge, the family against the city, knowledge through suffering (*pathei mathos*), law against conscience.

Summary of the drafted syllabus. The readings below are the ones named there.

The readings

The taught lesson, when it is written, is about half an hour on this site. The longer syllabus beside it — a language chapter, a double translation — is optional. You do not need it to read the lesson.

  1. Reading 1 Agamemnon

    Read

    Aeschylus, Agamemnon, in full. Focus on the watchman (1–39); the Hymn to Zeus and learning through suffering, pathei mathos (160–183); the sacrifice of Iphigenia (184–247); the carpet scene (905–974); Cassandra (1072–1330); Clytemnestra over the bodies (1372–1406).

    Look

    The Theatre of Dionysus at Athens, where the Oresteia was first staged in 458 BC.

    Questions

    • Why does Agamemnon walk on the tapestries?
    • Cassandra knows, and cannot be believed. What is the modern equivalent?
    • Is Clytemnestra a murderer, an avenging mother, or both?

  2. Reading 2 Libation Bearers

    Read

    Aeschylus, Libation Bearers (Choephori), in full. The recognition at the tomb (164–245), the great kommos (306–478), Orestes and Clytemnestra (892–930), Orestes seeing the Furies (1048–62).

    Look

    Orestes, Electra, and Pylades at Agamemnon’s tomb (Louvre K 428).

    Questions

    • The recognition by a lock of hair. Euripides later mocked it (Electra 518–44). Does it matter?
    • Can matricide ever be just?
    • What do the Furies represent?

  3. Reading 3 Eumenides and the Birth of the Court

    Read

    Aeschylus, Eumenides, in full. The Furies at Delphi (1–234); Athena founds the Areopagus (470–89, 681–710); the trial and the vote (566–777); the Furies transformed (778–1047).

    Look

    Orestes at Delphi, a red-figure krater, British Museum GR 1917,1210.1, and the Black Fury Painter’s Orestes krater in Naples.

    Questions

    • From vendetta to verdict: what does the jury replace?
    • Athena’s casting vote, and Apollo’s argument about parenthood (657–66). How do we read them now?
    • For physicians: compare the procedure of the Areopagus with medical ethics boards and peer review.

  4. Reading 4 Antigone

    Read

    Sophocles, Antigone, in full. Creon’s edict (162–210); the Ode on Man, polla ta deina (332–375); Antigone’s defence of the unwritten laws (450–470); Haemon and Creon (631–765); Tiresias (988–1090).

    Look

    The theatre at Epidaurus, fourth century BC: acoustics and design.

    Questions

    • Hegel read Antigone as two rights in collision (Lectures on Aesthetics). Do you agree?
    • Deinos means both wonderful and terrible. What does the Ode say about human skill, and about medicine (“he has devised escapes from diseases,” 363–64)?
    • When should conscience override law?

  5. Reading 5 Oedipus the King

    Read

    Sophocles, Oedipus the King, in full. The plague of Thebes (1–150); Tiresias (300–462); Jocasta and the crossroads (707–862); the shepherd and the revelation (1110–1185); the messenger and the blinding (1223–1296).

    Look

    Oedipus and the Sphinx, an Attic red-figure kylix by the Oedipus Painter (470–460 BC), Vatican Museums, Museo Gregoriano Etrusco, inv. 16541.

    Questions

    • For physicians: the play opens with an epidemic and a ruler as diagnostician. Compare it with Thucydides’ plague in Module 3.
    • Aristotle’s peripeteia and anagnōrisis (Poetics 11). Where exactly do they happen?
    • Is Oedipus guilty?

  6. Reading 6 Euripides’ Medea

    Read

    Euripides, Medea, in full. Medea on women’s lot (214–266); Jason and Medea (446–626); the great monologue (1021–80); the dragon chariot (1293–1419). Aristotle, Poetics chapters 6 and 13, is optional.

    Look

    A calyx krater with Medea in the dragon chariot (c. 400 BC), Cleveland Museum of Art 1991.1, Lucanian, attributed to the Policoro Painter.

    Questions

    • Is Medea a victim, a monster, or Euripides’ indictment of Athens?
    • Compare the endings: the Oresteia reconciles, Oedipus goes into exile, Medea escapes. What does each say about the city?
    • Why did Athenians pay to watch suffering?

Mastery check

Pass this to unlock the next module.

  1. A comprehension quiz of 20 questions on the Oresteia, Antigone, Oedipus the King, and Medea. Terms include pathei mathos, hamartia, peripeteia, anagnōrisis, katharsis, chorēgos, and ekkyklēma. Pass mark 80 percent.
  2. Latin: 25 items from chapters 19–24, plus five review items. Pass mark 80 percent.
  3. Greek: 20 vocabulary and form items from Athenaze chapters 1–5. Read one textbook sentence aloud and translate it. Pass mark 80 percent.
  4. An unseen Latin double translation through chapter 24, plus one Greek sentence in both directions.
  5. Recitation: Antigone 332–341, the opening of the Ode on Man, in English, and line 332 in Greek.
  6. A commonplace essay of 600 words: “Law, conscience, and the professional: Antigone, Creon, and a hard case from your own field.”
  7. A sketchbook with three drawings: the plan of Epidaurus, the Oedipus kylix, and a tragic mask or a chorus.
