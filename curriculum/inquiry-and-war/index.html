Inquiry and War

How history begins, and what a city does with victory.

Setting the scene

This layer adds citizen and soldier to the foundation: the citizen who fights, Persian and Peloponnesian Wars.

The third layer is a citizen who is also a soldier. Herodotus carries the Persian wars from Croesus and Solon through Marathon, Thermopylae, and Salamis. Thucydides carries the war Athens fought with Sparta: Pericles, the plague, Melos, and Sicily. The man still has the honor and the household of the first two layers. What is new is a city that decides, and a war fought in that city’s name.

What failed, and what the next age learned. Hubris and imperial overreach. Persia’s invasion failed through overconfidence. Athens then turned a defensive alliance into an empire, treated Melos brutally in 416 BC, launched the Sicilian Expedition (415–413 BC), and lost the war in 404 BC. Democracy could be carried away by ambition and demagogues. Civic courage and voice need inner law and conscience.

The invention of history: inquiry (*historiē*), fortune and hubris, freedom and empire, power and necessity. The lesson sequence adds Sparta as the counterpoint to Athens.

Summary of the drafted syllabus. The readings below are the ones named there.

From the text

Short passages from this module’s readings, in public-domain translations.

Herodotus
Histories. Aubrey de Sélincourt, revised by John Marincola (Penguin); Tom Holland (Penguin, 2013); or The Landmark Herodotus, edited by Robert B. Strassler, translated by Andrea L. Purvis, which is recommended for the maps.
Thucydides
History of the Peloponnesian War. The Landmark Thucydides, edited by Robert B. Strassler (Richard Crawley’s translation, with maps); Rex Warner (Penguin); or Martin Hammond (Oxford World’s Classics).
References
Book and chapter numbers, which these editions share.
Latin
Wheelock chapters 13–18, or Lingua Latina caps. XIII–XVIII. The weekly load is rebalanced once Greek is added: a little less Latin, a little less looking, and Greek in their place.
Greek
The Greek strand begins here, in month 4. Athenaze, Book I (Balme, Lawall, and Morwood; Oxford University Press, 3rd ed., 2016): the introduction plus chapters 1–3. Book I has 16 chapters, each split into α and β. The chapter titles are ὁ Δικαιόπολις, ὁ Ξανθίας, and τὸ ἄροτρον.

The readings

The taught lesson, when it is written, is about half an hour on this site. The longer syllabus beside it — a language chapter, a double translation — is optional. You do not need it to read the lesson.

  1. Reading 1 Why Did They Fight? Croesus and Solon

    Read

    Herodotus 1.1–5 (the proem and the abductions), 1.6–33 (Croesus, Gyges at 1.8–13, Solon and Croesus at 1.29–33), and 1.86–91 (Croesus on the pyre).

    Look

    Croesus on the pyre, an Attic red-figure amphora attributed to Myson (c. 500–490 BC), Louvre G 197.

    Questions

    • “Call no man happy until he is dead” (1.32). Is that useful to a doctor?
    • What does Herodotus mean by historiē?
    • How does Herodotus apportion blame for the war?

  2. Reading 2 Marathon

    Read

    Herodotus 5.97–105, the Ionian Revolt in brief, and 6.94–120: Marathon, Pheidippides (6.105–06), Miltiades, and the battle (6.111–17).

    Look

    The Soros, the burial mound of the Athenians at Marathon (490 BC).

    Questions

    • Why did Marathon matter so much to the Athenian self-image?
    • How does Herodotus handle the stories he doubts?
    • Pheidippides’ run: myth and evidence.

  3. Reading 3 Thermopylae and Salamis

    Read

    Herodotus 7.101–105 (Xerxes and Demaratus on Spartan law), 7.201–239 (Thermopylae), and 8.40–96 (Salamis, Themistocles, Artemisia at 8.87–88).

    Look

    The Serpent Column, the Plataean victory offering from Delphi (479 BC), now in the Hippodrome at Istanbul. And the trireme Olympias, a modern full-scale reconstruction launched in 1987.

    Questions

    • Demaratus says the Spartans have a master, the law (7.104). What is freedom under law?
    • Themistocles: genius, or opportunist?
    • Why give Artemisia, a woman in command, such prominence?

  4. Reading 4 The Spartan counterpoint: why Sparta’s constitution worked, and why it failed

    Read

    Coming soon. The lesson sequence places this after Thermopylae and Salamis: why Sparta’s constitution worked, and why it failed. Sparta’s mixed constitution gave stability and discipline, but a shrinking, closed citizen body resting on helots could not last. The works named for it are Aristotle’s Politics, Paul Cartledge’s Sparta and Lakonia, Herodotus, Plutarch’s Lycurgus, Polybius, Carl J. Richard’s The Founders and the Classics, Thucydides, and Xenophon’s Constitution of the Lacedaemonians. Chapter ranges are not set here.

    Look

    No object is assigned for this lesson yet.

    Questions

    • What did Sparta’s mixed constitution hold in place?
    • What did a shrinking, closed citizen body, resting on helots, make impossible to keep?

  5. Reading 5 The Truest Cause

    Read

    Thucydides 1.1–23: the Archaeology, his method at 1.20–22 (“a possession for all time”), and 1.23.6, the truest cause. Then 1.66–88, the debate at Sparta: the Corinthians on the Athenians (1.70), Archidamus, Sthenelaidas.

    Look

    The ostracon of Themistocles, and the Pnyx, as Athenian democracy at work.

    Questions

    • Thucydides on speeches (1.22.1): he wrote what he thought was required. Is that history?
    • Fear, honour, and interest (cf. 1.75–76). Are they still the motives of states?
    • The Corinthians’ portrait of the Athenians (1.70): an admirable people, or a dangerous one?

  6. Reading 6 Pericles, the Plague, and Civil War

    Read

    Thucydides 2.34–46, Pericles’ Funeral Oration; 2.47–54, the plague, including the clinical description at 2.48–53; and 3.82–84, civil war at Corcyra, where words changed their meanings.

    Look

    A bust of Pericles, a Roman copy after Kresilas, in the British Museum, and the Parthenon, built under Pericles (447–432 BC).

    Questions

    • For physicians: compare the plague account (2.48–53) with a modern case history. Symptoms, contagion, the collapse of norms.
    • The Funeral Oration’s ideal of Athens: an ideal, propaganda, or both?
    • Corcyra: how do crises corrupt language?

  7. Reading 7 Melos and Sicily

    Read

    Thucydides 5.84–116, the Melian Dialogue (“the strong do what they can,” 5.89); 6.8–26, the Sicilian debate, Nicias against Alcibiades; and 7.84–87, the catastrophe at the Assinarus and the quarries.

    Look

    Return to the trireme Olympias. What did the Sicilian expedition actually put to sea?

    Questions

    • The Melian Dialogue: are the Athenians wrong, or only honest?
    • Alcibiades against Nicias. What makes a leader persuasive, and what makes one wise?
    • Herodotus or Thucydides: whose history would you rather trust?

Mastery check

Pass this to unlock the next module.

  1. A comprehension quiz of 20 questions on Herodotus 1 and 6–8 and Thucydides 1–3 and 5–7. Terms include historiē, hubris, nemesis, isonomia, and stasis, plus the truest cause and the Melian Dialogue. Pass mark 80 percent.
  2. Latin: 25 items from chapters 13–18, plus five review items. Pass mark 80 percent.
  3. Greek: write the alphabet with accents and breathings, transliterate five words and read three aloud, and answer 15 vocabulary items from Athenaze chapters 1–2. Pass mark 80 percent.
  4. An unseen Latin double translation through chapter 18, plus one Greek sentence in both directions.
  5. Recitation: Thucydides 2.37.1, the opening of Pericles’ account of the constitution, in English, and Herodotus 1.1 in Greek script.
  6. A commonplace essay of 600 words. Either “Thucydides’ plague narrative as a clinical document: what can a modern physician learn from it?” or “Is the Melian Dialogue right about power?”
  7. A sketchbook with three drawings: the Croesus amphora, the Parthenon façade, and your own map of Salamis or Syracuse.
