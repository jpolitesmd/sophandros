Twelve weeks · In outline

Founders Edition

Read what the American founders read: the Greek and Roman sources of the republic, in their own words and languages, and walk the buildings they raised to them.

A first door, or a bridge

It is for the same reader as the main course, especially Americans curious about the classical roots of the Constitution. Twelve weekly units, about three months, at four to five hours a week. You can pause at any point.

The outline recommends building it once and using it twice: as a standalone edition, an easier first purchase, and inside the main course as a bridge after Phase II, Rome, since nearly all of its sources are Roman. Someone who continues gets credit for the Latin already done.

The week is the same engine. Read the primary texts in translation, with a short guide. Translate, by Ascham’s method, a short Latin passage the founders knew. Look at a building, a seal, or a painting. Reflect, in about three hundred words: what did the founders take, and what did they change?

The scholarship underneath

Three books are the spine, and the disagreements among them are part of the teaching.

  • Carl J. Richard, The Founders and the Classics: Greece, Rome, and the American Enlightenment (Harvard University Press, 1994), and his Greeks and Romans Bearing Gifts (2008).
  • Bernard Bailyn, The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution (Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, 1967). Bailyn argues that classical references were often illustrative, and that the English Commonwealth radicals, such as Trenchard and Gordon’s Cato’s Letters, shaped the Revolution’s thinking more directly. That counterweight is taught openly.
  • Gordon S. Wood, The Creation of the American Republic, 1776–1787 (1969).

The ancient sources

  1. Unit 1 The Mixed Constitution

    Polybius, Histories Book 6, and Aristotle, Politics, on the polity and the middle constitution. Look: the Roman Forum and the Senate house.

  2. Unit 2 Cicero, the Republican Statesman

    De Re Publica, including the Dream of Scipio, and De Legibus, on natural law. Translate a short passage of Cicero.

  3. Unit 3 Duty and Virtue

    Cicero, De Officiis, and Sallust, The War with Catiline, on virtue and corruption. Look: portrait busts of the Republic.

  4. Unit 4 Lives as Models

    Plutarch, Lives: Publicola, Cato the Younger, Lycurgus, Solon. Livy I–II on the founding of the Republic, and Cincinnatus in Book 3. The reflection asks why the name Publius.

  5. Unit 5 Tyranny and Liberty

    Tacitus, Annals I and Agricola. Look: the Arch of Titus.

The bridges

  1. Unit 6 Locke and Montesquieu

    Locke, Second Treatise of Government. Montesquieu, The Spirit of the Laws, on the separation of powers, with Book XI on England and Rome.

  2. Unit 7 Cato on Stage and in Print

    Joseph Addison, Cato: A Tragedy (1713), and Trenchard and Gordon, Cato’s Letters. Recite lines from the play.

The American founding

  1. Unit 8 Declaration and Debate

    The Declaration of Independence, and the reading of Jefferson and Adams.

  2. Unit 9 Publius and the opposition

    The Federalist, numbers 10, 14, 18–20 on ancient confederacies, and 39, 47, 51, and 63 on the Senate and the ancient republics. The pseudonym Publius, after Publius Valerius Publicola in Plutarch. The Anti-Federalist essays signed Brutus and Cato.

  3. Unit 10 John Adams’s Defence

    A Defence of the Constitutions of Government of the United States of America (1787–88), the selections on Polybius and the ancient republics.

  4. Unit 11 The Image of the Republic

    Washington as Cincinnatus, retiring in 1783, and the Society of the Cincinnati. The mottoes of the Great Seal: E pluribus unum; Annuit coeptis, set beside Virgil, Aeneid 9.625 and Georgics 1.40; Novus ordo seclorum, set beside Eclogue 4.5. Translate the Virgil lines.

  5. Unit 12 Architecture of the Republic

    Jefferson’s Virginia State Capitol, modelled on the Maison Carrée at Nîmes. Monticello. The University of Virginia Rotunda, modelled on the Pantheon. The United States Capitol, and Constantino Brumidi’s The Apotheosis of Washington (1865) in the dome. The capstone is an essay or a recorded oration: “Was America a Roman republic?”

Latin, and a little Greek

Latin is the core: a compressed start in Wheelock chapters 1–10, or Lingua Latina chapters 1–8. The double-translation texts are Latin the founders knew: Cicero in De Re Publica and De Officiis, Livy on Cincinnatus, the Virgil behind the Great Seal, and the Seal’s own mottoes.

Greek is light and optional. The alphabet, and a political vocabulary: dēmokratia, politeia, aristokratia, tyrannos, nomos, with short passages from Polybius 6 and Aristotle’s Politics beside a translation.

An optional journey

About seven to nine days, with a specialist operator, still only an outline: Philadelphia (Independence Hall and the National Constitution Center); Williamsburg and Richmond, including Jefferson’s Virginia State Capitol; Charlottesville (Monticello, and the Lawn and Rotunda at the University of Virginia); Washington (the Capitol and the Apotheosis, the National Archives, Mount Vernon). An optional extension of four or five days adds Rome, the Forum, the Pantheon, and the Capitoline, with Nîmes and the Maison Carrée as a short further stop.

This edition is an outline, not an open course. Pricing has not been set.
