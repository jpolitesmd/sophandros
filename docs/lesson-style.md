# Lesson style

The course builds one figure, layer by layer, as Greek society grows more
complex. The six Greek layers are honor, mind and home, citizen and soldier,
conscience and law, the examined mind, and practical wisdom. Rome continues
that figure into duty and law. The Renaissance continues him into the courtier
and the modern professional. Layer names are the owner’s frame. Module titles
and readings stay the drafted syllabi.

A lesson is taught on the site. The primary text is in the reader. A book is
optional, under “Go deeper,” and is never required to start.

## Voice

Second person, warm and authoritative. “Let’s begin with…” and “Notice that…”
belong here. Explain the context, then the passage, then what it means. Short
sentences. Define a Greek term when you first need it.

Quote a public-domain translation verbatim, with the work, the line numbers,
the translator, the year, and a link. Butler’s *Iliad* (1898) is the English
already verified for Homer. Do not invent a quotation. If a fact is uncertain,
say so in a code comment and keep it out of the prose.

## Shape of every lesson

1. **What you’ll learn.** Four claims the reader can say back.
2. **Today we add ___ to the foundation.** The blank is the piece named in the
   outline of 16 October 2026. Module 1 runs from “the world this honor stands
   in” through compassion. Each module also has a history lesson, Week 0.
3. **The teaching.** A short essay: the scene, the ideas, and why they matter
   to a modern professional. A module’s first page also sets the historical
   scene in the teacher’s voice.
4. **The reader.** The public-domain text, with Greek where it has been
   verified, and a glossary on the hard names.
5. **An illustration** with a caption and a real public-domain credit.
6. **Word of the day.** One Greek or Latin word from the reading.
7. **Check yourself.** Three to five questions. The answer is hidden until the
   reader asks for it, and it only restates the lesson.
8. **A short reflection.** A few sentences, stored in this browser. The lesson
   stands if the reader skips it.
9. **Key takeaways.** Four to six bullets, then one paragraph. Tie the new
   piece to the layers already laid. If this is the first layer, say that
   nothing of the man is under it, and name what it cannot do alone. The
   prologue is the ground under that layer, not a layer itself.
10. **References.** Chicago notes-bibliography form, from `src/data/references.ts`.
    Do not type a citation by hand. An entry the bibliography marks as unchecked
    stays marked “To verify.”

## What a lesson may assume

The reader has the previous lesson, and nothing else. Do not require a second
translation, a commentary, or a grammar book for the main points.

Works of art named in the syllabus get a caption: what the object is, the date
the syllabus gives, and the collection. They are not occasions for an open essay.
