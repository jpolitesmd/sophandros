What it is

The progression

A classical education was a way of watching human ideals change, and of watching the inventions and paradigm shifts that followed them. You see why a form prospered and why it failed, and you come to understand why we do what we do today. The American Founders used these books that way when they argued about a republic: what to take from the old constitutions, and what to change. Sophandros revives that education for a working life now.

The reading keeps the old order. Greece builds one figure, layer by layer, as that society grows more complex. You start with honor: the warrior, in the Bronze Age world of the Iliad. Then a mind and a home. Then a citizen who also fights. Then a conscience and a law. Then a mind that examines itself. Then practical wisdom, with Aristotle and the Parthenon.

Rome carries that person into duty and law. The Renaissance carries him into the courtier, and into the professional you already are. Each lesson is on this page, and the primary text is in the reader beside it. The shorter path through the founding is the Founders Edition.

Who it is for

People whose weeks are already full

It is for a professional who wants the sequence behind the work itself: why a practice, a law, or a building looks the way it does. The course was shaped around a physician in training, someone who can give the work a few hours, set it down, and pick it up again. Several of the Greece questions ask what a text has to say to medicine, and to any craft that judges particular cases.

You want Homer, Plato, Virgil, and Palladio, and enough Latin and Greek to meet a sentence in the original. The stated aim is modest. By the end you should be able to read adapted Caesar and Cicero, and Virgil with notes. Full fluency is not the promise.

The arc

Three phases

Four parts. Greece is the ideals, from a warrior’s honor to practical wisdom, and the prologue and the first chapter can be read now. Rome is duty and law. The Renaissance is what a later age made of that inheritance. The Birth of an American Nation is the culmination. The later lessons are still to be written.

How it works

Four steps, on this site

A lesson takes about half an hour. You are not sent out to buy the book first. The text is already on the page.

  1. 1

    Read the lesson here

    A short essay tells you the context, the ideas, and why they matter.

  2. 2

    Study the primary text in the reader

    The public-domain translation is on the same page, with a note on the hard words.

  3. 3

    Practice the Latin or the Greek

    One word, and a line to say aloud. A grammar book is optional.

  4. 4

    Check yourself

    A few questions with the answers revealed, then a short reflection.

The languages

One word, on the lesson

Each lesson gives you a Greek or Latin word from the reading, and a line to say aloud. That is the practice. You can finish Lesson 1 with nothing but this site.

A grammar book is a way to go further, not a requirement. If you want one later: Wheelock’s Latin is grammar first, and Ørberg’s Lingua Latina per se Illustrata is reading first. Greek, when you come to it, can use Athenaze. None of those books is how you start.

Optional

A tutor, a cohort, a journey

The lesson stands on its own. A grammar book, a double translation in the manner of Ascham’s Scholemaster (1570), and a tutor’s review of that translation are later extras. So are the three things below.

Not to learn Principles but to enlarge Experience.
John Milton, Of Education (1644), on seeing other countries

Milton wants a journey to enlarge experience, after the reading. If you take one, it closes a phase. It is not how a lesson begins.

A tutor. Thirty to forty-five minutes a month, or every fortnight, with a classicist who marks translations and can write a quarterly note.

A cohort. A live monthly seminar of eight to twelve, on the text of the module.

Travel, as a capstone. A short Athens leg after Greece. Rome and Naples after Rome. Then, if you wish, a Giro of fourteen to twenty-one days through Florence, Rome, the Veneto, and Venice, with an established operator. None of it is required to finish the course.

There is also a shorter outline, the Founders Edition: twelve weeks on the Greek and Roman reading behind the American republic. And an elective still unwritten, Physic: Hippocrates, Galen, and Vesalius.
