What you will learn

  • Why honor is the first layer of the complete man, and what the Greeks meant by it: timē, the worth others award you, and kleos, the name that outlives you.
  • How a quarrel over a prize sets the whole Iliad in motion, and why Achilles can name the wrong done to him but cannot settle it.
  • Where honor alone fails: it has no court above it, and it does not know when to stop.
  • How the poem ends: with compassion for an enemy, the first sign of a layer honor cannot supply by itself.

We begin with the man this course is building, at the smallest circle there is: himself. Before he can keep a household, serve a city or govern a republic, he has to stand for something. The first thing the Greeks thought a man should stand for was his honor.

Our text is Homer's Iliad, one of the oldest works of Western literature and, for more than a thousand years, the book at the center of every Greek education. It was composed for performance by singers who worked without writing, and was probably set down in the eighth or seventh century BC; the date is debated. It tells of a few weeks in the tenth year of the Greek war against Troy. We read it in Samuel Butler's translation of 1898, which uses the Roman names of the gods: when you see Jove, read Zeus; Juno is Hera; Minerva is Athena.

I. The quarrel

Iliad, Book 1

The poem tells you its subject in its first word. In Greek it is μῆνιν, mēnin: anger, the wrath of a man who has been wronged.

μῆνιν ἄειδε θεὰ Πηληϊάδεω Ἀχιλῆος
οὐλομένην, ἣ μυρί᾽ Ἀχαιοῖς ἄλγε᾽ ἔθηκε

Sing, O goddess, the anger of Achilles son of Peleus, that brought countless ills upon the Achæans. Many a brave soul did it send hurrying down to Hades, and many a hero did it yield a prey to dogs and vultures, for so were the counsels of Jove fulfilled from the day on which the son of Atreus, king of men, and great Achilles, first fell out with one another.

Iliad 1.1–7. Greek from the Perseus Digital Library; English by Samuel Butler, 1898.

Notice the order of the facts. First the anger; then its cost, counted in dead men; and only then its cause, a falling-out between two leaders. Homer wants you to judge the quarrel by what it costs before you hear who was right.

The story is quickly told. Agamemnon, the commander of the Greek army, has taken a captive girl, Chryseis, as his prize. Her father, a priest of Apollo, comes to ransom her; Agamemnon sends him away with threats, and Apollo answers with a plague. When the seer Calchas reveals that the plague is Apollo's punishment for the insult to his priest, Agamemnon agrees to give the girl back, but only if he gets another prize in her place. He takes Briseis, the prize the army had given Achilles. There was already bad blood between the two men; now it breaks into the open.

To a modern reader this looks like a fight over property. To the men in that camp it was a fight over standing. A prize was public proof of what a man was worth; to take it back was to announce, in front of everyone, that he was worth less. That public worth is what the Greeks called timē, honor. Listen to how Achilles describes what has been taken from him:

I came not warring here for any ill the Trojans had done me. I have no quarrel with them. They have not raided my cattle nor my horses, nor cut down my harvests on the rich plains of Phthia… We have followed you, Sir Insolence! for your pleasure, not ours… You forget this, and threaten to rob me of the prize for which I have toiled, and which the sons of the Achæans have given me.

Iliad 1.152–162 (selections). Butler, 1898.

Achilles' complaint is precise. He did not come to Troy for his own sake; he came for Agamemnon. The prize was the army's acknowledgment of his work. Taking it back is not just a theft; it is a lie told in public about what he has done.

Then comes the most important moment in the book. Achilles reaches for his sword, and Athena, visible to him alone, stops him:

Cease, then, this brawling, and do not draw your sword; rail at him if you will, and your railing will not be vain, for I tell you—and it shall surely be—that you shall hereafter receive gifts three times as splendid by reason of this present insult. Hold, therefore, and obey.

He stayed his hand on the silver hilt of his sword, and thrust it back into the scabbard as Minerva bade him.

Iliad 1.207–221 (selections). Butler, 1898.

Achilles does not kill the king, but notice why. It is not because a law forbids it, or because a court will judge the case. A goddess intervenes. There is nothing above the two men that both of them will obey. The wise old Nestor asks both to yield; neither does. Achilles withdraws from the fighting, and his mother, the goddess Thetis, persuades Zeus to let the Trojans win until the Greeks restore her son's honor. The deaths promised in the first lines will be the price.

II. The embassy

Iliad, Book 9

The plan works. The Greeks are driven back to their ships, and Agamemnon, frightened, sends three men to Achilles with an extraordinary offer: gold, horses, the return of Briseis, seven cities, and one of his daughters in marriage. By the rules of the camp, honor has been restored with interest.

Achilles refuses. In one of the great speeches of the poem he begins to question the whole bargain. The coward and the brave man, he says, are held in equal honor and die alike; so what is the prize for? Then he reveals what he has known all along:

My mother Thetis tells me that there are two ways in which I may meet my end. If I stay here and fight, I shall not return alive but my name will live for ever: whereas if I go home my name will die, but it will be long ere death shall take me.

Iliad 9.410–416. Butler, 1898.

This is the second meaning of honor. Beyond timē, the worth others award you now, there is kleos, the glory a song can keep after you are dead. Achilles has been offered every kind of timē and has seen through it; the only thing that would make his short life worth it is kleos. The choice between a long life and a lasting name is the first great choice in Western literature, and it is still a choice many professionals face: a safe career, or a hard one that matters.

III. The death of Hector

Iliad, Book 22

Achilles returns to the war only when his closest friend, Patroclus, is killed by Hector, the greatest of the Trojans. Now his anger turns from Agamemnon to Hector. Hector, for his part, stays outside the walls of Troy when every other Trojan has fled inside. His father and mother beg him to come in. He stays because he is ashamed: he had refused good advice, his men had died for it, and he cannot face the Trojans. The Greek word is aidōs, the shame that keeps a man at his post.

Achilles kills him. As he dies, Hector asks one thing:

I pray you by your life and knees, and by your parents, let not dogs devour me at the ships of the Achaeans, but accept the rich treasure of gold and bronze which my father and mother will offer you, and send my body home, that the Trojans and their wives may give me my dues of fire when I am dead.

Iliad 22.338–343. Butler, 1898.

Achilles refuses and drags the body behind his chariot. This is where honor alone fails. It told Achilles what was owed to him. It told Hector to stand his ground. It did not tell either man when to stop, and with nothing above it, it has turned into savagery. Even the gods are disgusted.

IV. Priam and Achilles

Iliad, Book 24

The poem does not end with the victory. It ends at night, in Achilles' hut. Priam, the old king of Troy, has crossed the enemy lines alone to ransom his son's body.

King Priam entered without their seeing him, and going right up to Achilles he clasped his knees and kissed the dread murderous hands that had slain so many of his sons.

“Think of your father, O Achilles like unto the gods, who is such even as I am, on the sad threshold of old age.”

Thus spoke Priam, and the heart of Achilles yearned as he bethought him of his father. He took the old man's hand and moved him gently away. The two wept bitterly—Priam, as he lay at Achilles' feet, weeping for Hector, and Achilles now for his father and now for Patroclus.

Iliad 24.477–512 (selections). Butler, 1898.

Two enemies weep together, each for his own dead. Achilles gives back the body and grants eleven days of truce for the funeral. Nothing about the war has changed; Troy will still fall, and Achilles will still die young. But something has been added to the man. For the first time he sees his enemy as someone's father and someone's son, and the honor that began in a quarrel over a prize ends in compassion.

What honor builds, and where it fails

Honor is the foundation, and it is a real one. It gives a man a reason to take up someone else's cause, a standard others can see, and a name worth keeping. It is why Achilles can name exactly what was taken from him, and why Hector stands at the gate.

But by itself honor has two flaws. It depends on what other people think, so it can be given and taken away by whoever holds rank. And it has nothing above it: no court to settle a quarrel and no rule to say when vengeance is enough. The rest of this course adds what honor lacks, one layer at a time: self-command and a household in Chapter 2, a city and its law, a mind that examines itself, and the practical wisdom to govern all of them.

If you work in a profession, you already know a plain version of Book 1. Credit for your work is taken by someone with the rank to take it. You can name the wrong. You can withhold your effort. What the Iliad asks is the harder question: what would put it right, and what will it cost the people who depend on you if it is never resolved?

Who used it

Plutarch reports that Alexander the Great carried a copy of the Iliad, corrected by his tutor Aristotle, on campaign, and kept it under his pillow with his dagger, calling it a provision for the art of war (Plutarch, Alexander 8).

Word of the chapter

τιμή

timē, honor: worth as others see it

With it: κλέος (kleos), glory that outlives you; μῆνις (mēnis), the wrath of the wronged; αἰδώς (aidōs), the shame that keeps a man at his post.

Latin you will meet this week

  • quid pro quo: “something for something.” Agamemnon's offer in Book 9 is a quid pro quo; Achilles refuses it.
  • status quo ante: “the state in which things were before.” What the embassy tries, and fails, to restore.
  • bona fide: “in good faith.” Is Agamemnon's offer bona fide, or a way to save face?
  • ad hominem: “to the man.” An argument aimed at the person, not the point. Achilles' insults to Agamemnon in Book 1 are ad hominem.

The exercise: a chreia

The first exercise of the classical schools after the fable and the narrative was the chreia: a short essay on a memorable saying or deed. Ancient teachers set a fixed pattern, and students worked through it in order. About 300 words.

  1. Praise the person in a sentence.
  2. Restate the saying or deed in your own words.
  3. Give its cause: why it was right.
  4. State the contrary: what would have happened otherwise.
  5. Offer an analogy and an example from another time.
  6. Cite a witness: someone else who said the same.
  7. Close with a short exhortation.

Your deed: Achilles puts his sword back in its scabbard. Then write a second, shorter chreia on a moment at work when someone you know held back when they had every right to strike.

For your commonplace book

Copy by hand Priam's words, “Think of your father,” and the two sentences after them. Then write two sentences: who, in your own work, is someone's father or son whom you have been treating only as an opponent?

Check yourself

  1. What is the first word of the Iliad, and why does it matter?
    Show the answer

    Mēnin, anger. Homer names the subject before anything else and counts its cost in dead men before he names its cause.

  2. Why is losing Briseis more than a loss of property to Achilles?
    Show the answer

    She was the army's public award for his fighting. Taking her back announces in front of everyone that he is worth less: it is a loss of timē.

  3. What stops Achilles from killing Agamemnon, and what does that tell you about his world?
    Show the answer

    Athena, not a law or a court. There is nothing above the two men that both will obey.

  4. What are the two fates Achilles describes in Book 9?
    Show the answer

    Stay and fight, die young and win lasting glory (kleos); or go home and live long but be forgotten.

  5. How does the poem end, and what has been added to Achilles?
    Show the answer

    Achilles returns Hector's body to Priam and grants a truce. He has learned compassion for an enemy, something honor alone could not give him.

Key takeaways

  • Honor is the first layer of the man: public worth (timē) and lasting glory (kleos).
  • It gives a man a reason to fight and a name worth keeping.
  • It depends on others' opinion, so those with rank can take it away.
  • It has nothing above it: no court to settle a quarrel and no limit on vengeance.
  • The poem ends in compassion, pointing to the layers still to come: self-command, a household, a city and its law.
