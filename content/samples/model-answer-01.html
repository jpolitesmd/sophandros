Achilles sheathes his sword

Achilles was the best fighter of the Greeks at Troy, a man who had come to war for another man's cause and asked only that his work be honored.

When Agamemnon took from him the prize the army had given him, Achilles reached for his sword to kill the king in front of everyone. Athena checked him, and he pushed the blade back into its sheath.

He was right to hold back, because killing the commander would not have restored his honor. It would have split the army in the middle of a war, and it would have made Agamemnon's insult the last word on Achilles: a man who could not be wronged without becoming a murderer.

Had he struck, the Greeks would have turned on one another at the ships, and the Trojans would have had the victory without a battle. The quarrel would have been settled, but only in the way a fire settles a dispute over a house.

A surgeon who is insulted by a colleague in the middle of an operation does not put down the knife and walk out; he finishes the work and settles the quarrel afterwards. So, too, Washington at Newburgh in 1783 had an army that would have followed him against a Congress that had not paid it, and he held it back instead.

Odysseus, lying awake in his own hall among the men who were devouring it, said the same thing to himself: "Heart, be still, you had worse than this to bear."

When you are wronged in public, name the wrong, but keep your hand off the hilt. The sword can always be drawn later; it can never be put back once it has been used.

A colleague who held back

My supervisor is the most careful engineer in our office. Last spring a client blamed her, in a meeting with our director, for a delay the client had caused. She had the emails to prove it and said nothing. She was right, because a public correction would have cost us the client and won her nothing she could not win in private. The next morning she sent the director the emails and the client a revised schedule. Answer the fact, not the man: that is what she taught me.

Check the steps: praise (the first sentence), restatement, cause ("He was right because…"), contrary ("Had he struck…"), analogy (the surgeon) and example from another time (Washington, Chapter 21), witness (Odysseus, Chapter 2, quoted exactly), exhortation. The shorter chreia compresses the same pattern into a few sentences.
