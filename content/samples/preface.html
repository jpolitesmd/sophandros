Preface

For more than two thousand years, the people who led free societies were educated in roughly the same way. They read the same books: Homer and the Greek historians, Plato and Aristotle, Cicero, Virgil and Plutarch. They practiced the same graded writing exercises. They learned enough Latin and Greek to read the originals, and enough history to recognize their own situation in someone else's. The education did not make them good, and it did not make them right. But it gave them a common store of examples, a trained habit of argument, and a sense that the problems of their own day had been faced before, by people who had thought hard about them.

The American Founders were among the last great products of that education. When Madison sat down to design a constitution, he began by studying every failed confederacy in history. When Washington refused to become a king, he had in mind a Roman farmer who had done the same. The Declaration of Independence is an argument built the way the classical schools taught arguments to be built. None of this was ornament. It was how they thought.

Most of us were never offered that education. We were trained, often very well, for a profession, and we learned what our work required. What we missed was the wider frame: the long conversation about how a person should live, how a community should be governed, how to persuade, how to decide, and how to command oneself before commanding anyone else. This book is an attempt to give that frame back to people with full working lives, in the time they actually have.

It follows one idea. As civilization grew, the circle a person had to govern widened, from the self to the household, the city, the republic, the empire and the world, until the Founders used the whole inheritance to build a new nation. Each chapter takes one step outward and asks two questions of its texts: what did this add, and where did it fail? Then the last part of the book brings everything back to the reader's own work: your writing, your decisions, your leadership.

A few principles guided the writing. Every quotation is copied word for word from its source, never written from memory, and every translation is an older one in the public domain, so that you are reading the words a nineteenth-century or early twentieth-century reader would have read. Every chapter tries to judge its subject fairly: to give the achievements of Greece, Rome or the Founders the weight they deserve, and their failures the weight they deserve, without letting either crowd out the other. The past was not a gallery of heroes, and it was not a catalogue of crimes. It was people, under pressure, making choices we can learn from.

And every chapter ends with work to do. The old education was not a reading list. It was practice: copying, imitating, arguing both sides, writing and rewriting. The exercises in this book are the same drills, turned toward the problems you face now. Read the chapters; but do the exercises. That is where the reading becomes ability.
