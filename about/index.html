σοφός · ἀνήρ

The wise man

Sophandros is a coined name for a modern revival of the classical education. It joins two Greek words, and it is not itself a word of ancient Greek.

σοφός

sophos, wise.

The adjective is the skilled or the wise person. It is the first half of the name.

ἀνήρ

anēr, man. The genitive is andros.

In a Greek compound the word takes that stem, so the ending is -andros.

= Sophandros is the wise man: the figure this reading is forming.

The mark draws that person as a profile in gold, with the name set in classical capitals.

What the name is for

The revival is a way of understanding the progression of human ideals, and of the inventions and paradigm shifts that followed them. Read in order, the books show why a form failed and why another prospered, and why we do what we do today. A professional already has a long education. What it often leaves out is that progression.

The order is the old one: Homer before the historians, the historians before the tragedians, Plato before Aristotle, Greece before Rome, Rome before the Renaissance. The American Founders used this kind of reading when they argued about a republic, taking some things from the old constitutions and refusing others. That use is written out in the Founders Edition. The full course is the same habit, carried through to a working life now.

Sophandros sets the sequence at a working pace. It is modelled on the humanist education of the European nobility and on the Grand Tour, rebuilt as self-paced study. Online work runs through the year. Travel, when it happens, is a capstone, after the principles are already in hand.

The weekly writing is Ascham’s double translation, from The Scholemaster (1570). The order of subjects, words studied beside things, is Milton’s, from Of Education (1644). Both are described on the home page, and carried out in the Greece syllabus.
