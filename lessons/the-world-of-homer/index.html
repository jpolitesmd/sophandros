Part I · Chapter 1 · Lesson 1

The World of Homer

Today we add the world this honor stands in. The prologue is the ground under it: farming, Crete, and the palaces. The poem itself begins in the next lesson.

What you’ll learn

  • Mycenae and Pylos were palace kingdoms. Linear B is the writing of a palace economy, and that writing was lost.
  • Around 1200 BC those kingdoms collapsed. The centuries after were poorer, with fewer settlements. The outline calls this the Dark Age.
  • The epics came out of oral poetry: bards composing in performance, with formulas and epithets. When they were written down is debated.
  • The world the poems describe is not the same as the world of the poet’s audience. Greeks treated Homer as a shared textbook for centuries. This course begins there.

Today we add the world this honor stands in to the foundation.

Let’s begin before the quarrel

The next lesson will open the Iliad on a quarrel in a camp. Before you hear that quarrel, you need the ground it stands on. This course builds one man, layer by layer, as Greek society grows more complex. The first society is the simplest in the sequence: chieftains, war bands, and personal loyalty. Honor, the virtue of that society, is the first layer. It has nothing under it but the prologue’s ground and this history.

Palace kingdoms, then a collapse

The outline places this layer in the Bronze Age and at Mycenae, about 1600–1200 BC. Mycenae and Pylos are the palace kingdoms you should be able to name. Their records are in Linear B, a script that kept the accounts of a palace economy. The syllabus points you to the Lion Gate at Mycenae, in the UNESCO listing “Archaeological Sites of Mycenae and Tiryns,” and to the gold mask Schliemann called Agamemnon’s, from Grave Circle A, about 1550–1500 BC. The name is Schliemann’s. The mask is centuries older than the Trojan War as later Greeks told it. The poem is not a report of that mask.

Around 1200 BC the palace kingdoms collapsed. What followed, on the outline’s account, was centuries of fewer and poorer settlements. That stretch is the Dark Age of this course. Linear B, the palace writing, was lost with the palaces. A technology of memory had to be found again, and it was not the same writing.

A poem kept by the voice

The epics came out of oral poetry. Bards composed in performance. They had formulas and epithets, set phrases a singer can reuse, so a long story can be held without a book. The Homeric question, in plain terms, is how that singing became the poems we have: one poet, or many, and how performance relates to a written text. The outline says the epics were probably written down in the 8th–7th century BC, and it marks that date as debated. Treat the century as unsettled. Treat the fact of oral composition as the point.

Hold the gap. The poems describe an older world, of palaces and chieftains, than the world of the poet’s later audience. When you read Agamemnon’s camp, you are not looking at a day in the audience’s own city. You are looking at a world the song remembered. That is why a prize, a guest-gift, and a name in the song matter so much in the lessons that follow. They are how worth was stored when the palace writing was gone.

Why this is the first textbook

Later Greeks treated Homer as their shared textbook for centuries. This course takes that use as its reason for beginning here. A professional today inherits practices, laws, and buildings whose reasons are older than the office. The first reason in this sequence is a public name: what a man is worth in other men’s eyes, and what a song can keep after he is dead. The next lesson gives that idea its Greek names, timē and kleos, inside Book 1. Today you only need the ground. The invention this module is here to teach is the hexameter line and the formula, a technology of memory after writing had been lost.

The passage below is the opening of the poem, in Samuel Butler’s English of 1898, with the Greek of the same lines. It is the door, not yet the quarrel.

The text: the opening of the Iliad

Samuel Butler, 1898, the first paragraph of Book 1. The Greek is Iliad 1.1–7 from the Perseus Digital Library. The rest of Book 1 is the next lesson.

μῆνιν ἄειδε θεὰ Πηληϊάδεω Ἀχιλῆος οὐλομένην, ἣ μυρίʼ Ἀχαιοῖς ἄλγεʼ ἔθηκε, πολλὰς δʼ ἰφθίμους ψυχὰς Ἄϊδι προΐαψεν ἡρώων, αὐτοὺς δὲ ἑλώρια τεῦχε κύνεσσιν οἰωνοῖσί τε πᾶσι, Διὸς δʼ ἐτελείετο βουλή, ἐξ οὗ δὴ τὰ πρῶτα διαστήτην ἐρίσαντε Ἀτρεΐδης τε ἄναξ ἀνδρῶν καὶ δῖος Ἀχιλλεύς.

Iliad 1.1–7. Greek text, Perseus Digital Library. Butler’s English follows.

Sing, O goddess, the anger of Achilles son of Peleus, that brought countless ills upon the Achæans. Many a brave soul did it send hurrying down to Hades, and many a hero did it yield a prey to dogs and vultures, for so were the counsels of Jove fulfilled from the day on which the son of Atreus, king of men, and great Achilles, first fell out with one another.

Butler, 1898, on Wikisource

Word of the day

κλέος

kleos, glory that outlives you. That is this course’s definition for the layer, not a line quoted from a lexicon. The song is the thing that stores it. The first word of the poem, in the Greek above, is μῆνιν, the anger. You will learn that word properly in the next lesson. For today, say kleos once, and remember what the voice was for.

    Palace kingdoms of the Bronze Age. Linear B is the script of a palace economy. It was lost when the palaces collapsed.

  2. 2. What does the outline say happened around 1200 BC?

    The palace kingdoms collapsed. The centuries after had fewer and poorer settlements. The course calls that stretch the Dark Age.

  3. 3. What is the Homeric question, in the plain terms of this lesson?

    How poems composed in performance, with formulas and epithets, became the written epics. The 8th–7th century date for the writing-down is debated.

  4. 4. Why does the course begin with Homer?

    Later Greeks treated Homer as a shared textbook for centuries. The poem keeps a picture of worth from a world older than the poet’s audience.

A short reflection

In a few sentences, if you want them: what does your own work still keep by memory and reputation, once the official record is gone? The history above is the course’s account. The sentence is yours, and it stays in this browser.

Key takeaways

  • There is no layer of the man under this one. The prologue is the ground: palaces that rose and fell. This lesson is the world of chieftains and war bands, remembered after that fall.
  • Linear B stored a palace economy and was lost. Formula and hexameter stored a song. That is the invention this module is carrying.
  • The poems describe an older world than the world of their audience. Read the camp as memory, not as a diary of the poet’s city.
  • Homer is the first textbook of the course because later Greeks used him as one. The next lesson opens the book on honor itself.

You now have the ground: palaces, a collapse, a Dark Age, and a poem kept by the voice. Honor, in the next lesson, is what that poem thinks a man is worth.
