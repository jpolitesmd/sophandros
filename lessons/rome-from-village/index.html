Rome from Village to Republic

Today we cross from the Greek city to the Roman country. We watch a cluster of huts on the hills above the Tiber become a city, a kingdom, and then a republic that would outlast every Greek polis.

What you’ll learn

  • How Romans told the story of their own beginnings, from Aeneas to Romulus and Remus, and why they told it that way.
  • What archaeology actually shows about early Rome: the hut villages, the drained valley, and the first public spaces.
  • Who the seven kings were said to be, and what Rome really owed to its Etruscan and Greek neighbors.
  • How, by tradition, Rome threw out its last king in 509 BC and replaced him with two consuls elected every year.
  • Why legend and history must be kept apart in this lesson, and why the legends still matter to us.

Today we cross from the city to the country.

In Greece we built the man in widening rings: the warrior’s self, the householder’s home, the citizen’s city. The Greek city was a fine thing. It was also small, proud and quarrelsome, and it never learned to join with its neighbors for long.

Rome is the next ring. Its great idea was the patria, the fatherland: a country larger than any one town, held together by law, by duty and by a shared story. The Roman word for the duty a man owes to his gods, his family and his country is pietas. That is the layer this chapter adds.

But Rome did not begin as a country. It began as a few villages on a river crossing. This lesson tells how it grew, as far as we can know it.

One warning first. Almost everything Romans believed about their first 250 years was written down centuries later. Our main narrative, Livy’s History of Rome, was written under Augustus, around 700 years after the date Romans gave for their founding. So in this lesson I will keep two columns side by side: what Romans said happened, and what the evidence lets us say. Where they agree, good. Where they don’t, I’ll tell you.

1. The place: seven hills and a river

Look at the setting first. Rome sits about 25 kilometers (15 miles) inland from the sea, on the Tiber, the largest river of central Italy. At that point the river has a low island in it, which made it one of the easiest places to cross.

Around the crossing rise the famous hills. The Palatine and the Capitoline matter most for this lesson. Between them lay a low, wet valley that flooded when the Tiber rose. That valley would one day become the Forum, the heart of Roman public life.

The site had three gifts. It controlled the river crossing, so traffic between Etruria to the north and Latium and Campania to the south passed by. It had hills that could be defended. And it was near the salt beds at the river’s mouth; an ancient road from Rome, the Via Salaria, means “the salt road.”

It also lay on a frontier. To the north lived the Etruscans, a rich people of cities, metalwork and trade, whose language was not Indo-European and is still only partly understood. To the south and east lived the Latins, who spoke the language that became Latin. Rome was a Latin town on the edge of the Etruscan world. That position shaped everything that followed.

2. What Romans said: Aeneas, Romulus and Remus

Romans told their beginnings in two stages, and both are worth knowing, because Romans used them to teach what a Roman should be.

The first stage is Aeneas. In the Greek legend of Troy he is a Trojan prince who escapes the burning city. Roman tradition made him sail west to Italy and settle among the Latins. His son founded the town of Alba Longa in the Alban Hills. Virgil’s Aeneid tells this story, and our next lesson begins with its most famous image: Aeneas carrying his old father out of Troy on his shoulders, leading his young son by the hand. That is pietas in a single picture.

The second stage is Romulus and Remus. Centuries after Aeneas, so the story ran, a king of Alba was pushed off his throne by his brother. The usurper forced the old king’s daughter, Rhea Silvia, to become a Vestal priestess so she would have no children. She bore twins anyway, and named the war god Mars as their father.

The twins were set adrift on the Tiber. The basket came ashore below the Palatine, where a she-wolf suckled them until a shepherd found them and raised them. Grown up, they restored their grandfather and set out to found a city of their own.

The ending is dark. The brothers quarreled over where to build and who should rule, and asked the gods for a sign by watching birds. In the version Livy repeats, Remus mocked his brother’s new wall by jumping over it, and Romulus killed him. Livy (1.6–7) gives more than one account; in all of them, Rome begins with a brother’s blood.

Romans kept this ugly detail. They could have dropped it. Mary Beard, in SPQR, points out that the city’s founding story starts with fratricide, and that Romans later saw their own civil wars as an echo of it.

The traditional date of the founding is 21 April 753 BC. That exact year comes from calculations by Roman scholars of the first century BC, especially Varro. Other ancient writers gave other years. Treat 753 as a convention, not a fact. ⚑ The different ancient dates for the founding should be checked before quoting any of them.

3. How Livy handles legends

Before we go further, notice how our main historian treats this material. In the preface to his history, Livy admits that the stories before the city’s founding are “more suited to poetry” than to reliable record, and says he will neither confirm nor refute them. He adds that ancient peoples may be allowed to mix the human with the divine to make their beginnings more dignified. (Livy, Preface 6–7; paraphrased here. ⚑ Check the Foster translation before quoting directly.)

Then he says why the stories are worth reading anyway. History, he writes, offers examples of every kind, set out for all to see: models to copy and warnings to avoid.

That is the method of this chapter, and in fact of this whole course. Livy is not mainly asking, “Did it happen?” He is asking, “What kind of people does this story make us?” We will ask both questions, and we will try not to confuse them.

4. What the evidence says: huts on the Palatine

Now the second column. What can archaeology tell us?

People lived on the site of Rome long before 753. Finds of Bronze Age pottery show activity around the Capitoline and the Forum area in the second millennium BC. ⚑ Exact dates of the earliest finds vary by excavation report.

By the Iron Age, in the 9th and 8th centuries BC, there were small villages of huts on the hilltops. Archaeologists have found the cuttings in the rock of the Palatine where the posts of oval huts once stood. Clay urns shaped like these huts, used to hold the ashes of the dead, survive from cemeteries around Rome and the Alban Hills. They show us thatched roofs, a single doorway and a smoke hole.

The low valley between the hills was at first used for burials. Later, probably in the 7th century BC, it was drained and filled, and given a first beaten-earth or gravel surface. From then on it was a public meeting place rather than a graveyard. ⚑ The date and sequence of the first Forum surfaces are debated among excavators.

The great drain that carried water from the Forum to the Tiber, the Cloaca Maxima, was credited by Roman tradition to the Tarquin kings. Parts of the channel are still in use today, though what you can see is mostly later rebuilding.

So the evidence tells a story of slow growth: villages on separate hills that gradually joined into one community with shared spaces. That is more like the prologue’s story of Crete, household to clan to town, than like a single founding day. It is quite possible that “Romulus” is a later name given to a long process. ⚑ Some archaeologists, notably Andrea Carandini, have argued that excavated walls on the Palatine support a real 8th-century founding; most historians are more cautious.

5. A city open to outsiders

One part of the legend fits the evidence surprisingly well. Romans remembered their city as a mixed and open place.

Livy says Romulus opened an asylum, a place of refuge on the Capitoline, where men of any background could come and join the new town, free or slave, no questions asked (Livy 1.8). Short of wives, the Romans then invited their Sabine neighbors to a festival and carried off their daughters. When the Sabine men marched on Rome, the women threw themselves between their fathers and their new husbands, and the two peoples merged into one (Livy 1.9–13).

The second story is brutal, and we should say so plainly. But notice what both stories have in common. Rome is not born pure. It is born by taking people in.

That is very unlike Athens, which prided itself on its citizens being born from the soil of Attica. It points ahead to one of Rome’s most important habits, which we will study later in lesson 58: Rome kept extending its citizenship to outsiders, conquered peoples and even freed slaves. The circle of belonging widened. That habit, more than any battle, built the country.

6. The seven kings

Roman tradition counted seven kings between the founding and the Republic, ruling for a total of about 244 years. Here they are, as Romans remembered them:

  1. Romulus, the founder and warrior.
  2. Numa Pompilius, a Sabine, the peaceful king who gave Rome its religious calendar and priesthoods.
  3. Tullus Hostilius, a warlike king who destroyed Alba Longa.
  4. Ancus Marcius, who was said to have extended Roman control toward the sea.
  5. Tarquinius Priscus, the first Tarquin, an immigrant from Etruria.
  6. Servius Tullius, a reformer, by some accounts of humble or even slave birth.
  7. Tarquinius Superbus, “Tarquin the Proud,” the tyrant who was driven out.

Seven reigns averaging 35 years each is far too long to be believed as it stands. Most historians think the list is partly invented and partly a memory of real rulers, compressed and tidied. ⚑

Notice the pattern, though. The list reads like a lesson plan. A founder who fights. A lawgiver who teaches reverence. Kings who expand. And then a reformer, followed by a tyrant. Romans used their kings the way Greeks used Homer’s heroes: as examples of what to be and what to avoid.

Two details deserve a closer look.

First, Numa. Romans credited him with organizing the worship of the gods: the priests, the calendar of festivals, the Vestal Virgins who kept the city’s sacred fire. Plutarch wrote his life alongside that of the Spartan lawgiver Lycurgus. For Romans, Numa showed that a city needs reverence and law as much as it needs soldiers. We will return to this in lesson 52, on religion and the state.

Second, Servius Tullius. He was credited with the first census and with dividing citizens into classes by wealth, which decided both what military equipment a man had to provide and how much weight his vote carried. The details we have probably describe a later system read back onto him. ⚑ But the principle is important: in Rome, a man’s duties to the state were tied to his place in it.

7. Etruscan kings and a Greek world

The last three kings matter for a different reason. Tradition makes the two Tarquins Etruscans, and Servius is linked to Etruria too. In the 6th century BC Rome grew rich and large, and much of what it built looked Etruscan.

The great temple of Jupiter Best and Greatest on the Capitoline was said to have been begun by the Tarquins and dedicated in the first year of the Republic. Its foundations, of huge blocks of local stone, still survive in part beneath the Capitoline Museums. It was one of the largest temples in the Mediterranean world in its day. ⚑ Its exact dimensions are debated.

The symbols of Roman power had Etruscan roots too, by Roman tradition. The fasces, a bundle of rods with an axe, carried before magistrates. The ivory folding chair of office. The purple-bordered toga. Romans themselves said these came from Etruria.

And beyond Etruria lay the Greeks. Greek colonies dotted southern Italy and Sicily, from Cumae near Naples to Syracuse. Greek pottery reached Rome early, and Greek gods were matched with Roman ones. One of the oldest Latin inscriptions known comes from the Forum: a stone found in 1899 beneath the black paving called the Lapis Niger. It is very hard to read, but it seems to contain the word recei, an early form of regi, “to the king.” It probably dates to the 6th century BC. ⚑ Readings of the Lapis Niger text are disputed.

So early Rome was not a lonely village of farmer-soldiers. It sat at a crossroads of Latins, Etruscans and Greeks, borrowing freely. Later Romans liked to picture their ancestors as plain and rustic. The evidence suggests a more cosmopolitan town.

8. Lucretia and the end of the kings

Now we come to the story Romans told most often about liberty.

The last king, Tarquin the Proud, was a tyrant, so the tradition says. He seized power by murder, ruled without consulting the Senate, and put the people to forced labor on his building works. One night, during a siege, young nobles in his army argued over whose wife was the most virtuous. They rode home to check. The other wives were feasting. Lucretia, wife of Collatinus, was at home working wool with her maids.

A few days later the king’s son, Sextus Tarquinius, returned to her house as a guest and raped her. Lucretia summoned her father and her husband, told them what had happened, made them swear to avenge her, and killed herself (Livy 1.57–59).

Standing by was Lucius Junius Brutus, the king’s nephew, who had survived the tyrant by pretending to be dull. (Brutus means “stupid.”) He drew the dagger from Lucretia’s body and swore that he would drive out the Tarquins and that no one would ever again be king in Rome. The people rose. The king was shut out of the city and went into exile.

The traditional date is 509 BC. It is close, perhaps by design, to the year the Athenians expelled their own tyrants in 510.

How much of this happened? We cannot say. The story has the shape of a tragedy, and it has a Greek parallel. But something did change around the end of the 6th century: Rome ceased to be ruled by kings. And the story did real work for centuries afterward. It taught that a ruler who treats citizens’ households as his property has broken the bond that makes him legitimate. Nearly five hundred years later, another Brutus, Marcus, would be urged to live up to his ancestor; we will meet him at the Ides of March in lesson 73.

9. The new Republic: two consuls, one year

What replaced the king? Not democracy, and not a single ruler under another name.

The Romans called their new state the res publica, literally “the public thing,” the common business of the people. In place of the king they set two consuls, elected each year. Each could block the other. When their year was up, they went back to being private citizens and could be held to account.

Livy, opening his second book, makes the point exactly. The beginning of liberty, he says, was not that the consuls had less power than the kings, but that their power lasted only a year (Livy 2.1, paraphrased).

Think about how much that idea carries. Power was:

  • Divided, between two holders of equal rank.
  • Limited in time, to one year.
  • Accountable, because the holder would soon be an ordinary citizen again.

The council of elders, the Senate, continued and gained weight. Assemblies of the people voted on laws and elected magistrates. Over the next two centuries the commoners, or plebeians, would fight and bargain for a share of power with the old aristocratic families, the patricians. That is the subject of our next chapter.

Romans even had a word for the hatred of kings that came out of this story. The very title rex became poison in Roman politics. Julius Caesar would hold supreme power, but he still found it wise to refuse a crown in public.

A small, real object may connect to these first years. In 1977 archaeologists at Satricum, south of Rome, found a stone inscribed around 500 BC naming the companions of one Publius Valerius. Some scholars link him with Publius Valerius Publicola, whom tradition makes one of the first consuls. ⚑ The identification is possible but not proven.

10. A Republic under pressure

The young Republic had a hard start. Rome lost much of its regional power after the kings left. It fought the Latins and its Etruscan neighbors, especially the rich city of Veii across the Tiber, for more than a century. Livy fills these years with heroes: Horatius holding the bridge, Mucius Scaevola thrusting his hand into the fire, and Cincinnatus, the farmer called from his plough to save the state, who laid down his power and went home when the job was done. We will study those exemplary Romans in lesson 50.

Then, around 390 BC, a Gallic war band from the north defeated a Roman army and sacked the city. ⚑ The traditional (Varronian) date is 390 BC; Polybius’ reckoning gives 387/386. Livy ends his first five books there. Rome rebuilt, and in the 4th century put up a great stone wall around the city, the so-called Servian Wall. Despite the name, the stone circuit we can see dates from after the sack, not from King Servius.

From that low point, the Republic began to grow again: through Italy, then across the Mediterranean. But that is the story of later chapters.

11. Why this story matters for the man

What does all this have to do with the man this course is building?

In Greece, a man’s highest loyalty was usually his own city, and the city was small enough to know by sight. Rome asked for a loyalty to something larger and more abstract: the res publica, the common thing, which outlived every individual in it.

That loyalty had to be taught. The Romans taught it by stories. A father who carries his father out of the fire. A wife who chooses death over dishonor. A nephew who turns his family’s shame into an oath for liberty. A farmer who gives back power he could have kept.

Some of these stories are false. Some are cruel. But they show us how a people trained its sons in pietas, in duty to gods, to family and to country, and in a habit of suspecting any one man who grows too great. In the next lesson we will see that duty in its most famous form, in Virgil’s Aeneas.

Word of the day

res publica

Latin for “the public thing,” the common business of the people. It is the root of our word republic. Romans used it for their state as a whole, whatever its form. For this course it names the fourth ring: the country, shared by everyone in it and owned by no one.

Check yourself

Three to five questions on what you just read. Show the answer when you are ready.

  1. 1. Why does this lesson keep “two columns”?

    Because our main written sources, such as Livy, were written centuries after the events. We set what Romans said beside what the evidence lets us say, and mark where they differ.

  2. 2. What three advantages did the site of Rome have?

    A river crossing on the Tiber with a low island, hills that could be defended, and access to the salt beds near the river’s mouth, on a frontier between Latins and Etruscans.

  3. 3. What does archaeology show about Rome in the 8th century BC?

    Villages of oval huts on the hilltops, such as the Palatine, with cemeteries nearby; the valley between, later the Forum, was drained and given a public surface later, probably in the 7th century.

  4. 4. According to Livy, what was new about the consuls?

    Not that their power was smaller than the kings’, but that it lasted only one year. There were also two of them, each able to check the other.

  5. 5. How do the stories of the asylum and the Sabine women point ahead to later Roman history?

    Both describe a Rome born by taking in outsiders. Later Rome kept extending citizenship to allies, conquered peoples and freed slaves, which was unlike the closed Greek polis.

A short reflection

In a few sentences, if you want them: what story does your family, firm or country tell about its own founding? Which parts of it are history, which are legend, and what does the legend teach you to be? The sentence is yours, and it stays in this browser.

Key takeaways

  • Rome grew at a crossing of the Tiber, on a frontier between Latins, Etruscans and Greeks, and borrowed from all of them.
  • Romans told their origins through Aeneas and through Romulus and Remus. These stories were written down centuries later and taught duty more than they recorded fact.
  • Archaeology shows slow growth: hut villages on the hills, then a drained valley that became a shared public space.
  • Tradition counts seven kings; the list is part memory, part invention, and reads like a set of examples.
  • By tradition, in 509 BC Rome expelled Tarquin the Proud after the rape of Lucretia, and replaced the king with two consuls elected for one year.
  • The Republic’s founding idea was power divided, limited in time, and accountable: the beginning of the res publica.

This is the opening of the fourth ring, the country. The next lesson gives that ring its virtue: pietas, in Virgil’s Aeneas.

What failed, and what the next age learned

  • Kingship without restraint. The tradition remembers the last king as a man who ruled without the Senate and treated citizens’ households as his own. Carried forward: power must be divided, limited in time and answerable. Rome’s two annual consuls were its first answer, and America’s Founders studied it.
  • A state for the few. The early Republic replaced one king with a narrow circle of patrician families. Carried forward: a republic must widen its circle or face conflict from within. The plebeians’ long struggle for a share, the subject of the next chapter, is that lesson in action.
  • A city that could be sacked. The Gallic sack showed that liberty without strength is not safe. Carried forward: Rome built walls, reorganized its army and bound its neighbors to it in alliances, the start of the road from city to country.

Glossary

  • Patria: fatherland; the country to which a Roman owed duty.
  • Pietas: duty to gods, family and country; the virtue of this chapter.
  • Res publica: “the public thing”; the Roman state, and the root of republic.
  • Latium: the region of central Italy where the Latins lived, and where Rome stands.
  • Etruscans: a people of central Italy north of Rome, whose cities, art and religion strongly influenced early Rome.
  • Palatine: the hill above the Tiber where tradition placed Romulus’ city and archaeology finds Iron Age huts.
  • Forum: the valley between Rome’s hills, drained and paved to become the center of public life.
  • Cloaca Maxima: “the greatest drain,” which carried water from the Forum to the Tiber.
  • Rex: king; after 509 BC a hated title in Rome.
  • Consul: one of two chief magistrates elected each year in the Republic.
  • Senate: the council of elders, made up of leading men and former magistrates.
  • Patricians and plebeians: the old aristocratic families, and the commoners.
  • Fasces: a bundle of rods and an axe, carried before magistrates as a symbol of their power.
  • Lapis Niger: “black stone”; a black-paved shrine in the Forum, beneath which lies one of the oldest Latin inscriptions.
