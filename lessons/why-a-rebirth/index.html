Why a ‘Rebirth’?

Today we stand in the Italian cities of the 1300s and 1400s and ask a simple question with a complicated answer: why did some Europeans come to believe that the ancient world was being born again, here, in their own lifetimes?

What you’ll learn

  • Where the word “Renaissance” comes from, and who first described the age that way.
  • Why Italy, of all places, was ready for a revival of Greece and Rome: its cities, its money and its ruins.
  • How a few men, starting with Petrarch, came to see the centuries behind them as an age of “darkness,” and why that view was bold and unfair.
  • What actually changed: books, schools, art and the image of the educated man.
  • Why historians still argue about whether there was a Renaissance at all, and what this course takes from that argument.

Today we arrive in the reborn city.

At the end of Part II we watched Rome’s empire break apart in the West. The great ring we had built, the country and then the empire, shrank. Cities emptied. The Latin of Cicero turned slowly into the languages of Italy, France and Spain. Much learning survived, in monasteries, in Byzantium and in the Islamic world, as our bridge lesson showed. But the old public life of the ancient city faded.

Now, about a thousand years later, we come back to Italy and find something new. In a handful of cities, men are hunting for the lost books of Cicero, learning Greek from refugees, measuring the ruins of Rome and arguing that their own age can match the ancients.

They had a word for what they thought was happening. Not “Renaissance” yet. But the idea was the same: a rebirth.

This lesson asks why. It is a history lesson, so we will look at places, money and people. But it is also a lesson about an idea, because the Renaissance was partly a story people told about themselves. As with early Rome, we will keep the story and the evidence side by side.

1. The word: who called it a “rebirth”?

The French word Renaissance simply means “rebirth.” Its use for a whole period of history is fairly late.

In 1550 the painter and architect Giorgio Vasari published his Lives of the Artists. He described how painting, sculpture and architecture had declined after antiquity and then revived in Italy, beginning around the time of Giotto, about 1300, and rising to perfection in Michelangelo. Vasari used the Italian word rinascita, rebirth, for this revival.

So the idea of rebirth comes from the period itself. Italians of the 1500s already believed they lived after a revival.

The use of “Renaissance” as the name of an entire era came three centuries later. The French historian Jules Michelet titled a volume of his History of France (1855) La Renaissance. Five years later, in 1860, the Swiss historian Jacob Burckhardt published The Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy. That book, more than any other, fixed the image that most of us still carry.

2. Burckhardt’s big claim

Burckhardt argued that something new about human beings appeared in Italy. In the Middle Ages, he wrote, a man knew himself mainly as a member of a group: a people, a family, a guild, a church. In Renaissance Italy that veil lifted. Men began to see the state as something to be built by human skill, “a work of art,” and to see themselves as individuals. (Burckhardt, Part I and Part II, chapter 1; paraphrased. ⚑ Check the Middlemore translation before quoting directly.)

It is a powerful idea. It is also, historians now agree, too simple.

People in the Middle Ages had strong personalities and wrote about themselves. Augustine’s Confessions, written about AD 400, is one of the most personal books ever written. And Renaissance Italians were still deeply bound to families, guilds, parishes and patrons. Burckhardt drew a bright line where there was really a gradual shift.

Keep his claim in mind anyway. It will help us later. This course is about the formation of a man, and the Renaissance is the age when the formation of a man became, once again, a deliberate project, the way it had been for Greek and Roman educators.

3. Why Italy? The cities

If there was a rebirth, why did it start in Italy and not in Paris or London?

The first answer is cities. By 1300, northern and central Italy was one of the most urban places in Europe. Florence, Venice, Milan and Genoa were among the largest cities in Christendom. ⚑ Population figures before the plague are estimates; Florence is often put near 100,000.

Many of these cities governed themselves. From the 11th and 12th centuries they formed communes: sworn associations of citizens who elected their own officials and made their own laws, often in defiance of the emperor or a local bishop.

So here, a thousand years after Rome, we are back in Ring 3: the city. These were not nations or empires. They were city-states, small enough that a merchant might sit on the council that taxed him, and proud enough to compare themselves with Athens and Rome.

That comparison was natural. The Italian communes had councils, elected magistrates and factions. When a Florentine wanted to think about liberty, tyranny or civic duty, the best books on the subject were Roman. Cicero, Sallust and Livy had written about a city-republic much like his own.

4. Why Italy? The money

The second answer is money.

Italian merchants dominated Mediterranean trade. Venice and Genoa ran shipping to Constantinople, the Black Sea and the ports of the Levant. Florence made fine woollen cloth and became a center of international banking.

In 1252 Florence began minting a gold coin, the florin. It kept a steady weight of gold and became one of the trusted currencies of Europe. Florentine banking families lent to popes and kings across the continent. Some of those loans went bad: the great banks of the Bardi and Peruzzi failed in the 1340s, partly because the King of England did not repay them.

Money matters to our story for a simple reason. Books, schools, buildings and paintings cost money. Copying a manuscript by hand took months. Building a cathedral dome took decades. A revival of learning needs patrons who can pay, and Italy had them: merchants, bankers, guilds, princes and popes.

It also needs men with time. The sons of wealthy families could study grammar, rhetoric and history for years. And the cities needed men who could write a good letter, draft a treaty, argue a case and make a speech. That gave the old Roman training in eloquence a practical market again.

5. Why Italy? The ruins

The third answer is all around them: the past itself.

Italians lived among the remains of Rome. Aqueducts crossed the fields. Roman walls ringed their cities. In Rome itself the Colosseum and the Pantheon still stood, though the city was much shrunken. For most of the 14th century the popes lived not in Rome but at Avignon, in southern France, from 1309 to 1377, and Rome sank further. ⚑ Estimates of Rome’s medieval population vary widely.

To a thoughtful Italian, the contrast was painful. Here were the ruins of the greatest state the world had known, and here, among them, were feuding barons, cattle grazing in the Forum and a city without its bishop. Those ruins asked a question: how did we fall so far, and could we rise again?

Italy also had an unbroken tradition of Roman law. From about 1100 the city of Bologna became famous for teaching Justinian’s law code, which we met at the end of Part II. Notaries and lawyers across Italy worked in Latin every day. They did not need to discover Rome. They already lived in its paperwork.

6. Petrarch and the idea of the “dark” age

Cities, money and ruins made a revival possible. But a revival also needs an idea. That idea came largely from one man: Francesco Petrarca, or Petrarch (1304–1374).

Petrarch is the subject of our next lesson, so here we need only his central move. He loved the ancient Romans, Cicero above all, so intensely that he wrote letters to them, as if to friends. And he came to see the centuries between Rome’s greatness and his own time as an age of decline. Latin had become crude. The great books were lost or neglected. The light of antiquity had gone out.

In 1341 he was crowned poet laureate on the Capitoline hill in Rome, deliberately reviving what he believed was an ancient ceremony. The gesture said: the ancient world can live again, here, through us.

The historian Theodor E. Mommsen showed in a famous article in 1942 that Petrarch turned the old Christian image of light and darkness on its head. Earlier writers had seen pagan antiquity as dark and the Christian age as light. Petrarch spoke of the ancient world as the age of light, and of the time after it as darkness. ⚑ Mommsen, “Petrarch’s Conception of the ’Dark Ages,’” Speculum 17 (1942); not yet in the course bibliography.

From Petrarch’s idea descends our whole three-part scheme of history: an ancient age, a “middle” age, and a modern age that recovers the ancient. The very phrase “Middle Ages” assumes that those centuries were only a gap between two better times.

That was bold. It was also unfair, and we should say so plainly.

7. What the “dark” age actually kept

The centuries Petrarch dismissed had preserved almost everything he loved.

Nearly every Latin classic we read today survives because monks copied it, often in the time of Charlemagne, around AD 800. Cathedral schools and, from about 1200, universities in Paris, Oxford and Bologna taught logic, law, medicine and theology at a high level. Scholars in the Islamic world had translated and commented on Aristotle, and Latin translations of their work reached the West in the 1100s and 1200s. Thomas Aquinas built a vast system of thought on Aristotle a century before Petrarch was born.

Historians have even named earlier revivals. In 1927 the American Charles Homer Haskins wrote The Renaissance of the Twelfth Century. In 1960 the art historian Erwin Panofsky wrote Renaissance and Renascences in Western Art, arguing that there had been several smaller revivals of antiquity before the great one, but that the Italian Renaissance was different because it saw antiquity as a whole, at a historical distance. ⚑ Haskins and Panofsky are not yet in the course bibliography.

So what was new? Not the survival of the classics. What changed was the attitude. Medieval scholars used ancient texts as authorities and tools. The humanists wanted to read them as the ancients had written them, in their original words, in their original setting, and to live by them.

8. What the humanists did

The men who led this change called their studies the studia humanitatis, “the studies of humanity”: grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history and moral philosophy. We call them humanists. They were not, mostly, opposed to religion. Most were Christians. The word means people devoted to these studies, not people who reject God.

They did four things that matter for this course.

They hunted for books. Humanists searched monastery libraries across Europe for lost or forgotten texts. In 1416 Poggio Bracciolini found a complete text of Quintilian’s Education of the Orator at the monastery of St. Gall; in 1417 he found Lucretius’ On the Nature of Things. We will follow these hunts in lesson 92.

They learned Greek. For centuries few western scholars could read Greek. In 1397 the Byzantine scholar Manuel Chrysoloras began teaching Greek in Florence. More Greek scholars came west in the following decades, and more still after the Ottoman Turks captured Constantinople in 1453. Plato, almost unknown in the Latin West, could be read again.

They built schools. Humanist teachers such as Vittorino da Feltre at Mantua and Guarino da Verona at Ferrara trained boys in Latin and Greek, history and moral philosophy, together with physical exercise. This was the beginning of the education this course is modeled on. Its descendants were the grammar schools of Tudor England and, eventually, the schooling of America’s Founders.

They wrote for public life. Humanists served as chancellors, secretaries and ambassadors. In Florence the chancellors Coluccio Salutati and Leonardo Bruni wrote the republic’s letters and defended its liberty against the Duke of Milan. Around 1403–04 Bruni wrote a Panegyric to the City of Florence, praising it as the true heir of the Roman Republic. We study him in lesson 98.

9. The rebirth made visible

The revival was not only in books. It was in stone.

In 1420 Filippo Brunelleschi began the great dome of Florence Cathedral. No one in the West had built a dome so large since antiquity. Brunelleschi had studied the ruins of Rome, and he solved the problem with a double shell of brick laid in a herringbone pattern, built without the wooden support frame that would normally hold up an arch while it was built. The dome was finished up to the lantern in 1436. ⚑ Details of the construction method are still studied and debated.

Around the same years, in the 1410s and 1420s, Florentine artists developed linear perspective, a mathematical way to show depth on a flat surface. Leon Battista Alberti set it out in writing in On Painting (1435). Sculptors such as Donatello made free-standing bronze figures in the ancient manner.

Here was proof, visible from every street in Florence, that moderns could equal the ancients. That is why Vasari, a century later, could tell the story of art as a rebirth.

10. The argument about the argument

So was there a Renaissance? Historians still argue, and you should know the main positions.

One view, Burckhardt’s, says yes: a real break, the birth of the modern individual and the modern state, in Italy.

A second view says the change was gradual, with deep medieval roots, and that “Renaissance” mostly names the self-image of a small, learned elite. Most people in 1450 farmed, prayed and lived much as their grandparents had.

A third view, from the historians Anthony Grafton and Lisa Jardine in From Humanism to the Humanities (1986), looks hard at what humanist schools actually did. They argue that the education was often less inspiring than its own advertisements. A great deal of it was drill: memorizing, copying and imitating. And it suited the powerful because it produced polished, obedient servants for princes and republics.

This course takes something from each. Something real did change in how educated people saw the past and themselves. The roots were older than the humanists admitted. And an education built on imitation can produce a parrot instead of a man. That last risk is the subject of this chapter’s review, lesson 96: imitation versus judgment.

11. Why this matters for the man

Think back to what we have built. Greece gave us the self, the household and the city. Rome gave us the country and the empire, and the duty and law that hold them together. Then the empire broke, and the rings contracted.

The Renaissance shows us something important about history: it does not move in a straight line. Rings can shrink and grow again. Here they grow from the city once more, and this time also outward into a new ring: a “republic of letters,” a community of learned men across Europe who wrote to each other in Latin, shared their discoveries and argued about the ancients.

It also shows what a single idea can do. Petrarch could not command an army. He changed how Europe saw its own past, and through that, what it chose to teach its sons. Every lesson in this course, including this one, descends from that choice.

Word of the day

humanitas

Latin for “humanity,” in the sense of the qualities that make a person fully human: learning, kindness and refinement. Cicero used it, and the humanists took their name from it. For this course it names what the whole education aims at: not information, but a cultivated human being.

Check yourself

Three to five questions on what you just read. Show the answer when you are ready.

  1. 1. Who first used the word “rebirth” for the revival of the arts, and who made “the Renaissance” the name of an era?

    Giorgio Vasari spoke of a <em>rinascita</em> in his <em>Lives of the Artists</em> (1550). Jules Michelet (1855) and above all Jacob Burckhardt (1860) made “the Renaissance” the name of a period.

  2. 2. Give three reasons why the revival began in Italy.

    Its many large, self-governing cities; its wealth from trade, cloth and banking; and its daily contact with Roman ruins and Roman law.

  3. 3. How did Petrarch reverse the old image of light and darkness?

    Earlier Christian writers saw pagan antiquity as dark. Petrarch treated antiquity as the age of light and the centuries after it as darkness, the origin of our idea of the “Middle Ages.”

  4. 4. Why is the idea of a “dark” age unfair?

    Those centuries copied and preserved nearly all the Latin classics, built universities, taught law and logic, and recovered Aristotle through translations, including from Arabic.

  5. 5. What do Grafton and Jardine argue about humanist education?

    That it was often more drill than inspiration, and that it suited rulers because it produced polished, obedient servants; its own claims about forming free, virtuous men should be tested.

A short reflection

In a few sentences, if you want them: what in your own field has been “lost” and is worth recovering? And is the age you are tempted to call “dark” really dark, or only unfamiliar to you? The sentence is yours, and it stays in this browser.

Key takeaways

  • “Rebirth” was the period’s own idea (Vasari’s rinascita, 1550); “the Renaissance” as an era is a 19th-century name, made famous by Burckhardt in 1860.
  • Italy was ready because of its self-governing cities, its commercial wealth and its daily contact with Roman ruins and law.
  • Petrarch gave the movement its central idea: antiquity as light, the following centuries as darkness, and his own time as a recovery.
  • That idea was bold but unfair; the “Middle Ages” preserved the classics and had revivals of their own.
  • The humanists hunted books, learned Greek, built schools and wrote for public life; artists and architects made the revival visible.
  • Historians still debate how new the Renaissance was. This course takes the change as real, the roots as old, and imitation as a danger.

This reopens Ring 3, the city, a thousand years after Rome, and opens a new ring of learning across borders. The next lesson meets the man who started it: Petrarch.

What failed, and what the next age learned

  • An empire that could not hold. The fall of Rome in the West broke the great ring of the country and empire. Carried forward: civilization survived in smaller units, cities, monasteries and schools, which could rebuild when the time came.
  • Contempt for the recent past. The humanists dismissed the centuries that had saved their books. Carried forward: a sound education honors those who preserved what it loves, and judges each age on the evidence.
  • Learning for the few. The revival was the work of a narrow, wealthy elite, and its schools could train flatterers as easily as statesmen. Carried forward: printing, from about 1455, would carry the books far beyond that elite, and the best teachers tried to form judgment, not just style.

Glossary

  • Renaissance: French for “rebirth”; the revival of classical learning and art, beginning in Italy in the 1300s.
  • Rinascita: Italian for “rebirth”; Vasari’s word for the revival of the arts.
  • Commune: a self-governing association of citizens in a medieval Italian city.
  • Florin: the gold coin of Florence, first struck in 1252.
  • Humanist: a scholar of the studia humanitatis; not, in this period, an opponent of religion.
  • Studia humanitatis: grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history and moral philosophy.
  • Humanitas: the cultivated qualities that make a person fully human.
  • Middle Ages: the “middle” period between antiquity and its revival; a name that assumes a judgment.
  • Manuscript: a book copied by hand.
  • Linear perspective: a mathematical method for showing depth on a flat surface.
  • Republic of letters: the community of scholars across Europe who wrote to each other, mostly in Latin.
  • Avignon papacy: the period 1309–1377 when the popes lived at Avignon rather than Rome.
