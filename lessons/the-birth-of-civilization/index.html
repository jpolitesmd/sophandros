Prologue

The Birth of Civilization

Today we lay the ground beneath the foundation. The man this course builds has not appeared yet. The rings here are household, clan, town, and palace-state.

What you’ll learn

  • How people moved from hunting and gathering to farming, and why that change made everything else possible.
  • How farmers reached Crete, and how their households grew into clans, towns, and finally palace-states.
  • What the Minoan palaces were, what they achieved, and what we still do not know about them.
  • How Minoan civilization was damaged, taken over, and finally lost, and what that teaches.
  • Why this story is the ground beneath the foundation of Greek civilization, and of Homer.

Today we lay the ground beneath the foundation.

Before we build the man, we must understand the ground he stands on. In Module I.1 we will meet Achilles, the warrior who cared above all for honor. But Achilles did not appear out of nowhere.

Behind him lay thousands of years in which people learned to farm, to settle, to build, to trade, to write and to govern. Behind him, too, lay the memory of a great island civilization that rose and fell before the Greeks of Homer’s day were born.

That civilization is Minoan Crete, the first great civilization of Europe. Its story is short enough to tell in one lesson and rich enough to teach us something about every later age.

It also follows the pattern of this course. In Sophandros we follow a man as his circle of responsibility widens: from himself, to his household, to his city, to his country, and beyond. In this lesson we watch whole societies take those first steps: from the household, to the clan, to the town, to the palace-state.

Read this lesson straight through, like a chapter in a textbook. I will explain each stage, show you the evidence, and tell you plainly where scholars disagree.

1. Before farming: the hunter-gatherers

For almost all of human history, people lived by hunting animals and gathering wild plants. They moved with the seasons, following herds and ripening foods. Groups were small, a few families or a few dozen people, and everyone knew everyone. There were no kings, no cities and no writing.

That does not mean these people were simple. They made fine stone tools, sewed clothing, controlled fire, buried their dead with care and painted the walls of caves. They knew their landscapes in remarkable detail.

But their way of life set limits. You cannot store much food if you are always moving. You cannot build a large house if you will leave it next season. And you cannot support people who do not find their own food, such as full-time priests, craftsmen or rulers.

So the circle of responsibility was narrow: the self and the small band. That was about to change.

2. The Neolithic revolution: farming begins

About 11,500 years ago, after the last Ice Age ended, people in the Fertile Crescent began to farm. This is an arc of land in the Near East. It runs from the Levant (modern Israel, Palestine, Lebanon and western Syria) up into southeastern Turkey and down through Mesopotamia (modern Iraq).

Here grew the wild ancestors of wheat, barley, lentils and peas. Here, too, lived wild sheep, goats, cattle and pigs. Over many centuries, people learned to plant and replant these crops, and to tame and breed these animals.

Archaeologists call this the Neolithic revolution, from the Greek words for “new stone” age. “Revolution” is a little misleading, because the change took thousands of years and came to different places at different times. But its effects were revolutionary.

What farming made possible

Farming produced more food from the same land, and more food meant more people. Farmers had to stay near their fields, so they built permanent houses and villages. They stored grain, so they needed pots, granaries and ways to track what belonged to whom.

Some people could now spend their time on crafts, building or religion instead of finding food. The household, a family living in one house and working its own fields and animals, became the basic unit of life. In the language of this course, the second ring was born.

Two famous sites in Anatolia (modern Turkey) show how remarkable this period was.

Göbekli Tepe: monuments before cities

On a hilltop in southeastern Turkey stands Göbekli Tepe. From 1995 the German archaeologist Klaus Schmidt excavated large round and oval enclosures there. They are ringed by huge T-shaped stone pillars, some over five meters tall, carved with foxes, boars, snakes, vultures and scorpions. Some pillars have arms and hands carved on their sides, as if they stand for human or godlike figures.

The oldest enclosures date to roughly 9600–8200 BC. These dates come from radiocarbon testing, and ongoing digs keep refining them. Either way, that is thousands of years before writing, metal tools or the first cities.

Schmidt saw Göbekli Tepe as a sanctuary, where hunter-gatherers met for rituals and feasts. He argued that the work of building it may have pushed people to settle and farm. Later excavators have also found signs of houses and daily life there, so this “temple without a village” picture is now debated. Either way, Göbekli Tepe shows that people could organize large shared projects at the very start of the farming age.

Çatalhöyük: a town of households

Further west, on the Konya plain of central Turkey, lies Çatalhöyük, one of the largest early farming settlements ever found. People lived there from roughly 7100 to 6000 BC. James Mellaart first excavated it in the 1960s, and from 1993 a long project led by Ian Hodder took over.

Çatalhöyük had no streets. Its mud-brick houses were packed so tightly that people walked across the rooftops and climbed down into their homes by ladders. Houses were rebuilt again and again on the same spot.

Inside, people plastered and painted the walls, set cattle horns into benches and walls, and buried their dead beneath the floors. Several thousand people may have lived there at once, though estimates vary widely.

What Hodder’s team did not find matters just as much. There was no sign of palaces, of temples standing apart from houses, or of a ruling class, and the houses were much alike. Çatalhöyük was a town of households with no central government. People could live together in large numbers long before anyone ruled over them. Central power came later, and we will watch it arise on Crete.

3. Farmers cross the sea to Crete

Over the following millennia, farming spread out of Anatolia and the Near East. Some of the people who carried it went by sea.

Crete is a large, mountainous island in the southern Aegean Sea, about 260 kilometers long, lying between Greece, Turkey and North Africa. Its first lasting settlers arrived around 7000 BC. That date rests on radiocarbon from the lowest levels at Knossos and is still being refined. They settled at Knossos, in the north-central part of the island, on the spot where a palace would stand some five thousand years later.

They came as farmers

These settlers did not slowly become farmers on Crete. They arrived as farmers, bringing wheat and barley, and sheep, goats, pigs and cattle, animals not native to the island. So they came by boat, on purpose, carrying seed grain and live animals across open water. This was a planned migration by households who meant to stay.

Modern genetics supports this picture. In 2017 a team led by Iosif Lazaridis published a study of ancient DNA in the journal Nature (“Genetic origins of the Minoans and Mycenaeans”). They found that both the Minoans and the later Mycenaean Greeks descended mostly from early farmers related to those of western Anatolia.

The Minoans also carried some ancestry related to peoples further east, from the Caucasus and Iran. The Mycenaeans carried that too, plus an extra share related to peoples of the Eurasian steppe. In plain words: the people of Bronze Age Crete were largely descended from those first farming households who crossed the sea.

For the next three thousand years, Neolithic Crete was a world of villages. Knossos grew slowly, layer upon layer, into one of the largest Neolithic sites in the Aegean. Life centered on the household, its fields and its flocks.

4. Households, villages and clans: Early Minoan Crete

Around 3100 BC (dates vary by a century or so), the people of Crete began to use copper and then bronze, a mix of copper with tin or arsenic. Archaeologists call the time from then until about 2000 BC the Early Minoan period.

The name “Minoan” is modern. Sir Arthur Evans, the British archaeologist who excavated Knossos from 1900, named the civilization after Minos, the legendary king of Crete in Greek myth. We do not know what these people called themselves.

In this period a wider ring joined the household: the clan, a group of related families who shared ancestors, land and duties.

The tombs of the Mesara

The clearest evidence comes from the Mesara plain in southern Crete. Here communities built tholos tombs: round stone tombs with a single doorway, used for burial over many generations, sometimes for centuries. Hundreds of people might be laid in one tomb, with pottery, stone vessels, seals and ornaments. Outside there were often paved areas where the living seem to have gathered for rites.

A tomb used by one group for centuries tells us how people saw themselves. They were not only separate families but members of a lineage reaching back to the ancestors and forward to the unborn. Shared tombs bound families together and gave them a claim to their land. The circle had widened from the household to the clan.

Myrtos: one household or many?

At Myrtos (Fournou Korifi) on the south coast, the archaeologist Peter Warren excavated a small Early Minoan settlement in the 1960s. It was a cluster of about ninety rooms on a hilltop, destroyed by fire around 2200 BC.

Warren first read it as a single shared household of perhaps a hundred people, with common storage, cooking and weaving. Later scholars, especially Todd Whitelaw, read it as a hamlet of several separate households living side by side. The debate itself is useful: when do separate households become a larger community, and how can archaeologists tell?

Bronze, ships and the wider world

Bronze changed the economy. Tin does not occur on Crete, so it had to be imported, probably from far away (sources in the east are most often suggested). Copper came from places such as Cyprus and the Cyclades. To get metal, Cretans had to trade, and to trade they had to sail.

Crete sits in the middle of the eastern Mediterranean, and its people made the most of it. They traded with the Cyclades, the ring of small islands to the north famous for marble figurines. They traded with the coasts of Anatolia and the Levant, and eventually with Egypt.

Egyptian stone vessels and scarabs turn up on Crete, and Cretan goods turn up in Egypt. Later Egyptian texts and tomb paintings show people from a land called Keftiu, generally identified with Crete, bringing gifts or trade goods.

Trade brought wealth, new ideas and new needs. It also rewarded those who could organize it: collect goods, build ships, keep accounts and deal with foreigners. That is how the next ring came into being.

5. The first palaces: the palace-state (about 1900 BC)

Around 1900 BC, in what archaeologists call the Middle Minoan period, the Cretans built something new. Evans and later scholars called these large, complex buildings palaces. The first stood at Knossos, at Phaistos in the south and at Malia on the north coast; others followed.

More than a ruler’s home

Each palace was built around a large rectangular central court, with wings of rooms on every side. There were storerooms full of huge clay jars (pithoi) for grain, oil and wine, along with workshops, shrines, halls, grand staircases and upper floors. Most followed a similar plan, which suggests shared ideas about how such a building should work.

Most scholars agree that the palaces’ main economic job was redistribution: gathering goods in and handing them back out. Grain, oil, wine and wool from farms over a wide area flowed into the palace stores. The palace then fed craftsmen, officials and workers, paid for rituals and feasts, and traded the surplus abroad.

That made each palace the center of a territory, not just a town. We can fairly call these early palace-states: the fourth ring, after household, clan and town.

Writing we cannot read

A system like this needs records. The Minoans developed two scripts: Cretan hieroglyphic, used mainly on seals, and Linear A, written on clay tablets and other objects. Linear A seems to have been used mostly for accounts, lists of goods and amounts.

Because the later Linear B script borrowed from it, we can read some Linear A numbers and guess at some signs. But Linear A has not been deciphered, and we do not know what language it records. So when you meet confident claims about what Minoans believed or how they were governed, remember: we have never read a Minoan explaining it in his own words.

6. Destruction and rebuilding: the Neopalatial age (about 1700–1450 BC)

Around 1700 BC, the first palaces were destroyed. Many scholars blame a major earthquake, since Crete lies in an active earthquake zone and the damage was widespread. Some suggest conflict as a cause or a contributing factor.

What came next is remarkable. The Minoans rebuilt, and rebuilt grander. Archaeologists call this the Neopalatial (“new palace”) period, the height of Minoan civilization.

Art and daily life

The new palaces and the great houses (“villas”) around them were decorated with frescoes, paintings on wet plaster. They show processions, women in elaborate dress, lilies, monkeys, dolphins and the sea. Minoan art is known for its sense of movement and its love of nature.

One caution. Many famous Knossos frescoes survive only as fragments, and artists working for Evans in the early twentieth century heavily restored them, even partly reimagined them. What you see in the museum is often part ancient and part modern.

The palaces also show impressive engineering. Knossos had stone-lined drains, clay pipes, and light-wells that brought air and daylight into inner rooms. Evans called one suite the “Queen’s Megaron” and identified a room with a drain as a toilet (plausible, but debated in detail). Minoan builders clearly managed water and light with real skill.

Bull-leaping and religion

Bulls are everywhere in Minoan art. The best-known image, the Bull-Leaping Fresco from Knossos, shows figures grasping a charging bull’s horns and vaulting over its back. Was this a sport, a ritual or both, and was it ever done exactly as shown? Scholars debate it.

Stone bull horns (Evans called them “horns of consecration”) crowned buildings and altars. The double axe (labrys) appears as a sacred symbol.

Minoans worshipped in palace shrines, in caves and at peak sanctuaries on mountaintops, where people left clay figurines of people and animals. Female figures stand out in Minoan art, including the famous faience “snake goddess” figurines from Knossos. These too were partly restored, and some “Minoan” ivory figurines in museums are now thought to be modern forgeries.

Many scholars think one or more goddesses held a central place in Minoan religion. Without readable texts, though, we cannot know their names or beliefs.

The island world of Thera

North of Crete lies the volcanic island of Thera (modern Santorini). In 1967 the Greek archaeologist Spyridon Marinatos began excavating a Bronze Age town there, at Akrotiri, buried in volcanic ash and preserved like Pompeii.

Its houses, some three stories high, held wonderful frescoes of ships, fishermen, boxers and landscapes, deeply shaped by Minoan culture. Akrotiri shows that the Minoan world reached across the southern Aegean.

Who ruled?

Here we reach one of the great open questions: we do not know who ruled Minoan Crete.

Evans imagined a priest-king, a “Minos,” ruling from Knossos, and named rooms to match (“Throne Room”). Others have proposed a priestly class, a council of leading families, several independent palace-states, or a queen or priestesses, since women appear so often in ritual scenes.

Compared with Egypt or the Near East, Minoan art shows remarkably few images of a single ruler or of war. Some scholars take this to mean Minoan society was peaceful. Others point out that defensive sites and weapons do exist, and that a lack of evidence does not prove a lack of war. The honest answer: the palace-state is clear, but its government is not.

7. The eruption of Thera

At some point in the Neopalatial period, Thera’s volcano erupted in one of the largest eruptions of the last several thousand years. It buried Akrotiri, though its people seem to have left in time, since few bodies have been found. It threw ash across the eastern Mediterranean and may have sent tsunamis against Crete’s north coast.

When did it happen?

The date is one of the great debates of Aegean archaeology. Radiocarbon dating, including an olive branch buried by the eruption, has pointed to roughly 1630–1600 BC, in the later seventeenth century. Older dating, based on links with Egyptian history, put it later, around 1500 BC.

More recent radiocarbon work suggests a date in the late seventeenth or the sixteenth century BC. The question is not settled, so treat any single date with caution.

Did it end the Minoans?

In 1939 Marinatos proposed that the eruption destroyed Minoan Crete. But the palaces carried on long afterward, perhaps for several generations. Most scholars now think the eruption weakened Crete, by damaging coasts, fleets, harvests or trade, rather than destroying it outright. The final blows came later.

8. Destruction and takeover (about 1450 BC)

Around 1450 BC, fire destroyed many sites across Crete: palaces such as Phaistos and Malia, and villas and towns across the island. Knossos alone continued as a palace. The cause is debated: earthquake, conflict between Cretan centers, or invasion.

What happened next is clearer. The culture of Knossos changed. Warrior burials with weapons appear around it, and pottery and art shift toward styles of the Greek mainland. The palace scribes also began writing in a new script, Linear B.

Linear B: Greeks at Knossos

Linear B was adapted from Linear A, but it records an early form of Greek. The English architect and codebreaker Michael Ventris proved this in 1952 when he deciphered it.

The thousands of Linear B tablets from Knossos are inventories: sheep and wool, chariots, armor, land, and offerings to gods. Some of those gods, such as Zeus, Poseidon and Athena in earlier forms, are familiar from later Greek religion.

The people now running Knossos were Greek speakers from the mainland: the Mycenaeans, named after their great citadel of Mycenae. So the Minoan palace-state did not simply vanish. A more warlike people took it over, kept its system of administration, adapted its writing to their own language, and ran it for their own purposes.

9. The final fall

The Mycenaean palace at Knossos was itself destroyed later, probably in the fourteenth century BC. This date, and the date of the Knossos tablets, is among the most argued questions in the field: some scholars say around 1375–1350 BC, others later. Life on Crete went on, but the great palace system was over.

The wider collapse

By around 1200 BC, the whole eastern Mediterranean was in crisis. Within a few decades, the palace kingdoms of mainland Greece, the Hittite empire in Anatolia and many cities of the Levant were destroyed or abandoned. Egypt survived, but weakened.

The historian and archaeologist Eric Cline describes this in 1177 B.C.: The Year Civilization Collapsed. He argues that no single cause explains it. Instead, earthquakes, drought, famine, migration, raiding and the breakdown of trade together overwhelmed a tightly connected system. On Crete, people moved to hilltop settlements that were easier to defend, such as Karphi high in the Lasithi mountains.

Writing vanished from Greece and Crete for several centuries, and the Linear scripts were forgotten. When the Greeks wrote again, they used a new alphabet borrowed from the Phoenicians, as we will see in Module I.2.

10. From Crete to Mycenae and Homer

What survived was memory. Later Greeks told of King Minos, who ruled the seas from Knossos, and of the Labyrinth built by the craftsman Daedalus. They told of the Minotaur, half man and half bull, to whom Athenian youths were sacrificed until Theseus killed it.

Homer’s Iliad names Knossos and the Cretan leader Idomeneus among the Greeks who sailed to Troy. The Odyssey, in Book 19, is said to call Crete a land of ninety cities; that line is not quoted here until it has been checked against the text.

These are legends, not history. Yet the myths remember bulls, a palace so large one could get lost in it, and a sea power centered on Knossos. Archaeology has found all three.

The Mycenaeans who took over Knossos are the world Homer’s poems remember, dimly and centuries later. In the next lesson, The World of Homer, we turn to them: their citadels, their warriors, their collapse, and the poets who kept their memory alive. Out of that world comes Achilles, and the first layer of the man: honor.

Neolithic

From the Greek words for “new” and “stone.” It is the name archaeologists give the age of the first farming societies. The change was slow. The effects were not. The household, in the language of this course, begins there.

A short reflection

In a few sentences, if you want them: where does your own work depend on a few centers that collect and hand out what everyone else needs? The history above is the course’s account. The sentence is yours, and it stays in this browser.

Key takeaways

  • Farming changed everything. The Neolithic revolution in the Fertile Crescent and Anatolia created the settled household, stored wealth, and the population growth that made civilization possible.
  • Civilization grew ring by ring. On Crete we can watch it widen: household, then clan (the shared tholos tombs), then town, then palace-state.
  • The palaces were economic centers. Minoan palaces gathered and redistributed produce, supported crafts, and ran trade, and needed writing (Linear A) to keep accounts.
  • Minoan Crete reached a high level of art and engineering, but much remains unknown, including its language and who ruled.
  • Civilizations can be damaged, taken over, and lost. Earthquake, possibly the Thera eruption, destruction around 1450 BC, Mycenaean takeover, and the wider collapse around 1200 BC ended Bronze Age Crete.
  • Memory outlived the civilization. Greek myths of Minos and the Minotaur kept a distorted memory alive, and Homer’s world grew from the Mycenaean heirs of Crete.

This is not yet a layer of the man. It is the ground under the first layer. The next lesson sets that ground in the world Homer remembers.

What failed, and what the next age learned

  • Centralization was brittle. When so much depended on a few palaces that collected and redistributed goods, the destruction of those centers brought down the whole system. Carried forward: a society needs strength spread across households and communities, not concentrated in one place. The Greek polis, with its citizen-farmers, was in part a different answer.
  • Wealth without defense invited takeover. However we interpret the scarcity of war in Minoan art, Crete’s wealth ended up in the hands of the more warlike Mycenaeans. Carried forward: prosperity must be protected. Homer’s world begins with warriors, and the man’s first layer is the courage to defend what he values.
  • Civilization can be lost. Writing, palaces, and trade networks disappeared for centuries after about 1200 BC. Carried forward: nothing achieved is permanent. Each generation must learn, keep, and pass on what it has received, which is the purpose of an education like this one.

Glossary

  • Neolithic: the “new stone age,” the period of the first farming societies.
  • Neolithic revolution: the shift from hunting and gathering to farming and herding.
  • Fertile Crescent: the arc of land from the Levant through southeastern Turkey to Mesopotamia where farming first developed.
  • Anatolia: the Asian part of modern Turkey.
  • Bronze Age: the period when bronze (copper plus tin or arsenic) was the main metal for tools and weapons; in the Aegean, roughly 3100–1100 BC. That span is debated.
  • Minoan: modern name, from King Minos, for the Bronze Age civilization of Crete.
  • Mycenaean: the Bronze Age civilization of mainland Greece, named after Mycenae.
  • Tholos tomb: a round, stone-built tomb; on Crete, used by a group over many generations.
  • Palace: in Minoan archaeology, a large complex around a central court that served as an economic, religious, and administrative center.
  • Redistribution: a system in which produce is collected by a center and then handed out to support workers, rituals, and trade.
  • Pithos (plural pithoi): a large clay storage jar.
  • Linear A: an undeciphered Minoan script.
  • Linear B: a script adapted from Linear A to write early Greek; deciphered by Michael Ventris in 1952.
  • Fresco: painting on wet plaster.
  • Labrys: a double axe, a Minoan sacred symbol.
  • Peak sanctuary: a mountaintop shrine.
  • Keftiu: an Egyptian name for a foreign land, generally identified with Crete. The identification is debated.
  • Neopalatial: the “new palace” period of Crete, about 1700–1450 BC. Those dates are debated.
